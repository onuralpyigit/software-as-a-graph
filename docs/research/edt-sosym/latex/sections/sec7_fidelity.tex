\section{Fidelity Assessment}
\label{sec:fidelity}

Fidelity, the degree to which the virtual entity reproduces its physical counterpart, is among the least operationalised characteristics of digital twins~\citep{jones2020characterising}. For an architectural twin, the question is concrete: when the same fault hits the twin's simulation and the counterpart, do they show the same damage? This section defines what we compare (\S\ref{sec:dimensions}), what such a comparison can and cannot show (\S\ref{sec:independence}), and how we fix the analysis before seeing results (\S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{Three fidelity dimensions}
\label{sec:dimensions}

All comparisons are computed from two traces in the shared schema of \S\ref{sec:s2m}: one simulated, one observed. Each fidelity report records its tolerances. It is a report and never a gate (R6), because a gate on an unvalidated fidelity metric would turn the metric's noise into release decisions.

\paragraph{Steady state.} Five metrics are computed, each as a per-element error against a declared tolerance (Table~\ref{tab:tolerances}):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item the publication rate of each topic;
  \item the delivery rate of each reader--topic pair;
  \item the p50 and p95 latency of each topic;
  \item the share of samples that miss their deadline.
\end{itemize}
The report gives the median error and the share of elements within tolerance.

\label{tab:tolerances}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
Metric & Tolerance \\
\midrule

\paragraph{Fault response.} For a fault on component $v$, the \emph{observed impact} $\Iobs(v)$ follows the definition of the simulator's $\Idyn$: the pre-fault minus the post-fault delivery rate, summed over surviving readers. The faulted component's own receipts are excluded, the value is not clamped, and the simulator's guard band is used. Computing $\Iobs$ from a trace with the simulator's definition makes the two directly comparable. On the ATM scenario, $\Iobs$ computed from a simulated trace differs from the engine's internal $\Idyn$ by at most 0.0002. At 1\,Hz on the smallest scenario the difference is about 0.03, because whole samples are counted. Two comparisons follow:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item rank agreement between simulated and observed impact across faulted components, measured by Spearman's $\rho$ and top-$K$ Jaccard;
  \item for each single fault, the Jaccard index between the sets of readers that stall in simulation and in observation (the \emph{stall set}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Middleware status.} When the simulator runs with DDS semantics, it maintains the DDS status counters for every application entity:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item liveliness alive and not-alive counts;
  \item requested and offered deadline misses;
  \item lost and rejected samples.
\end{itemize}
The report compares, for each counter, the set of entities with non-zero values on each side and the per-entity counts. The broker relays' own endpoints exist only on the counterpart, so they are excluded, and their number is reported.

\paragraph{From fidelity report to closed-loop feedback.} Two Tier-2 advisory diagnostics connect this section back to calibration (\S\ref{sec:s2m}). First, \texttt{detect\_drift} evaluates steady-state errors against the tolerances of Table~\ref{tab:tolerances} and flags drifted metrics, topics and subscribers when the share within tolerance drops below a configured threshold (default 80\%), signalling when incremental EMA re-calibration (\texttt{update\_profile}) is warranted. Second, \texttt{diagnose\_fault\_residuals} inspects Tier-1 versus Tier-2 stall-set mismatches and large per-component impact residuals; when an observed Tier-2 fault trace is available, it re-infers trigger policies for mismatched applications and proposes non-mutating profile updates (\texttt{apply\_residual\_feedback}). Both diagnostics are surfaced in the hybrid verification report and remain strictly advisory (never blocking CI gates, R6).

\subsection{Independence analysis: what the counterpart can show}
\label{sec:independence}

The counterpart is generated from the same model, and partly by the same functions, as the simulators' configuration. Some agreement is therefore guaranteed by construction, and reporting it as fidelity would be circular. This is the conceptual-validity trap that the simulation-validation literature warns about~\citep{sargent2013verification}. Before any study, we classified every part of the counterpart by whether it is independent of the simulator (Table~\ref{tab:independence}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Which parts of the counterpart are independent of the simulator. Only agreement on the independent parts counts as fidelity evidence.}
\label{tab:independence}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}Y l Y@{}}
\toprule
Counterpart part & Independent? & Consequence \\
\midrule
DDS delivery, discovery and liveliness; OS scheduling; real elapsed time; container kills and network partitions & yes & what fidelity results can speak to \\
Topic rates, mean service times, queue bounds, failover rule, placement & no (same plan) & agreement is not evidence \\
Trigger policies (reactive behaviour) & no (shared function) & agreement between a reactive simulator and a reactive counterpart is circular \\
Late-sample handling & no (agents are told the rule) & a counterpart in the simulator's own late-drop mode is never treated as truth \\
Application logic & no (stubs) & no result covers real application behaviour \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The table bounds every claim the twin can make from its counterpart. \emph{The strongest attainable conclusion concerns timing and transport fidelity on an emulated system under declared behaviour.} No outcome can show that a production system behaves this way. We regard producing this table as a necessary step whenever a twin's physical side is itself generated from the twin's model, a situation that pre-deployment twins cannot avoid. To prevent these boundaries from staying prose-only, the hybrid verification service (\S\ref{sec:services}) emits structured, machine-readable \texttt{caveats} in every \texttt{hybrid\_report.json}---automatically flagging reactive policy circularity (unless \texttt{--open-loop} is selected), rate-scaled latency distortion when $\kappa < 1$, Cyclone DDS post-partition rediscovery lag, station-less slowdown divergence, in-process transport omission and any skipped unsupported probes.

\subsection{A pre-registered fidelity study}
\label{sec:protocol}

Fidelity studies have many forks: which scenarios, which faults, which simulator configuration, which metric, which threshold. Each fork is a chance to find agreement after the fact. We therefore wrote the analysis down, including the expected likely outcome, before running any comparison, and froze it in the repository on 16 November 2026 (commit \texttt{9f84aad9}), before any study number existed~\citep{sag-software}. The only runs made before freezing were pilot runs that measure capacity and noise; none was compared with a simulator. We summarise the protocol here. The complete document lists every exclusion rule, rerun rule and amendment procedure. The operational hybrid verification workflow (\texttt{hybrid-verify}, \S\ref{sec:services}) is built on the same primitives for practitioner triage and gray-failure probing, and is strictly separate from this frozen confirmatory study: its runs never enter Table~\ref{tab:study}.

\paragraph{Population and capacity.} The unit of analysis is the \emph{scenario}. Components, seeds and repetitions are not independent units, and treating them as such would be pseudo-replication. The candidate population has twelve scenarios: eleven generated domains and the ATM scenario. The counterpart runs Python stubs on a single machine, so scenarios whose busiest host exceeds a measured capacity are rate-scaled. Every topic rate is multiplied by $\kappa \le 1$, and every service time and deadline is divided by $\kappa$. This preserves utilisation, but not the ratio of transport delay to service time. Latency results therefore count only at $\kappa = 1$, and scenarios need $\kappa \ge 0.2$ to enter. A scenario also enters only if, in a fault-free run, its rate error stays within 5\% and its CPU p95 stays within 80\% of one core in every container. Both conditions are decided before any fault run. Load ramps on two pilot scenarios outside the population set the capacity at $B_h = 3{,}033$ messages per second for the busiest host, with CPU as the binding limit. Nine of the twelve candidates pass the gate, all at $\kappa = 1$, so latency results count for every included scenario. Three are excluded: the autonomous-vehicle scenario saturates one container (CPU gate), the enterprise scenario fails the rate gate because six of its hosts never discover their peers within the run, and the financial-trading scenario would need $\kappa < 0.2$.

\paragraph{Amendments.} Two amendments were registered during the sweep, both before any simulator arm ran and before any comparison with $\Iobs$ was computed. The first corrected a validity check: on one host of the industrial-control scenario, every application is a warm standby that emits nothing while its primary is alive, so a log-recency check rejected 31 of the first 80 runs of a healthy host by chance. The amended check judges such hosts by container liveness. The second withdrew that scenario rather than re-running it from scratch (about 400 runs), a decision about cost taken without knowledge of any outcome. The study therefore has $n = 8$, not the nine that passed the gate. No seed, target, arm, margin, threshold or metric changed.

\paragraph{Observed conditions.} The confirmatory condition (O-open) makes every application periodic. This removes behaviour as a source of difference between simulator configurations, because the trigger policy is shared (Table~\ref{tab:independence}). The reactive condition is reported descriptively only.

\paragraph{Arms.} Seven arms are compared against $\Iobs$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{S0}, the published queue-flow configuration;
  \item \textbf{S1}, S0 with DDS deadline semantics;
  \item \textbf{S2}, the current model: S1 plus host stations, per-process randomness and replica awareness;
  \item \textbf{S3}, S2 calibrated from a separate fault-free run;
  \item \textbf{S4}, S2 with reactive behaviour, scored in the reactive condition only;
  \item \textbf{R}, a closed-form first-order reference with no simulation, ranking a publisher by the declared rate it contributes to each topic times the topic's readership;
  \item \textbf{N}, S2's scores with shuffled targets, as an instrument check.
\end{itemize}
The data used for calibration never come from evaluated runs (R5 vs.\ R6, \S\ref{sec:requirements}).

\paragraph{Hypotheses.} The primary hypothesis is \textbf{H1}: under O-open, S2 ranks publishing applications closer to $\Iobs$ than S0 does. It is tested by a one-sided exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\citep{wilcoxon1945individual} on per-scenario differences in Spearman $\rho$, at $\alpha = 0.05$. The analysis reports a Hodges--Lehmann effect size with a confidence interval and a TOST equivalence test against a margin $\delta$ derived from a noise pilot. A decision table fixed in advance maps the test results onto four headlines: \emph{better}, \emph{equivalent}, \emph{inconclusive} and \emph{worse}. A secondary, Holm-corrected family asks four questions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{H2}: does S0 clear an absolute rank-agreement level of 0.70?
  \item \textbf{H3}: does S2 beat its own first-order reference R?
  \item \textbf{H4}: does S0 beat R?
  \item \textbf{H5}: does calibration reduce per-reader delivery error (S2 vs.\ S3)?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Noise ceiling.} No simulator can be expected to agree with the counterpart better than the counterpart agrees with itself. Every per-scenario $\rho$ is therefore reported next to a noise ceiling: the mean leave-one-repetition-out rank agreement of $\Iobs$ with itself. In the noise pilot (20 publishing applications, three repetitions) this ceiling was 0.90, with a standard deviation of 0.007. Three publishers had clearly high impact; the other 17 had $\Iobs$ between 0.014 and 0.038 but were still ordered consistently across repetitions. The equivalence margin follows from this pilot by a rule fixed before its value was known: $\delta = \max(0.05, \mathrm{SD}) = 0.05$. An earlier pilot run on the same scenario gave a ceiling of only 0.56, with the 17 low-impact publishers reordered in every repetition; its traces were not archived and the difference is unexplained. The protocol therefore does not assume the pilot's ceiling but measures it again on every included scenario. It also records \emph{inconclusive} as a possible outcome in advance, because other scenarios may be noisier than the pilot and a study of nine (in the event, eight) scenarios tolerates only a few losses (\S\ref{sec:discussion}). An inconclusive result is to be reported as such, and never as evidence that the two configurations are equal.

\paragraph{What each result permits.} The protocol pairs each outcome with the claim it licenses. For example, a \emph{better} H1 licenses ``the current model matches emulated rankings better under declared behaviour''. It does not license ``labels are more accurate for real systems''. A failed H3 licenses ``against this counterpart, simulation adds no ranking value over a closed-form formula''. It does not license ``simulation never adds value''. We regard such a claim table as part of a twin's fidelity specification, not as a statistical nicety. It is what keeps a fidelity number from being quoted in a context it does not cover.
