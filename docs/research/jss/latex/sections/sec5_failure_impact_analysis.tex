\section{Failure-Impact Analysis via Heterogeneous GNN and Interpretable Forecasting}\label{failure-impact-analysis-via-heterogeneous-gnn-and-interpretable-forecasting}}

\label{sec:5}

Quality attribution (Section~\ref{sec:4}) tells an architect why a component is structurally
critical. This section asks the complementary question: \emph{how much of the system actually fails}
when a given component fails, and how well each predictor anticipates it. We define the three
simulation oracles that supply ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:5.1}), the two predictors we evaluate
against them (Section~\ref{sec:5.2}), the independence between predictor inputs and the label path
that makes the evaluation sound (Section~\ref{sec:5.3}), and two checks that take node type seriously
(Section~\ref{sec:5.4}).

\hypertarget{ground-truth-three-simulation-oracles}{%
\subsection{Ground Truth: Three Simulation Oracles}\label{ground-truth-three-simulation-oracles}}

\label{sec:5.1}

In the absence of runtime telemetry, ground truth is produced by discrete-event failure simulation
over the \emph{raw} structural graph \(G_{\text{structural}}\) --- directly on
\texttt{PUBLISHES\_TO}, \texttt{SUBSCRIBES\_TO}, \texttt{ROUTES}, \texttt{RUNS\_ON},
\texttt{CONNECTS\_TO}, and \texttt{USES} edges, without the derived \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} projection.
For each component \(v\), a failure is injected, the resulting disruption is propagated over a fixed
horizon, and the residual service degradation is measured.

\textbf{The framework contains three such oracles, and they are not interchangeable.} We name them
here because which one backs a given number materially bounds what that number can support.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\(I^*(v)\)} --- produced by \texttt{FaultInjector}: the mean subscriber feed-loss fraction
  under a breadth-first cascade. This is the label the learned predictors are trained and evaluated
  against, and it backs the predictor tables of Section~\ref{sec:8.1}.
\item
  \textbf{\(I_{\text{comp}}(v)\)} --- produced by \texttt{FailureSimulator}: a four-component weighted
  composite,
  \(I_{\text{comp}}(v) = 0.35\,\text{reachability\_loss} + 0.25\,\text{fragmentation} + 0.25\,\text{throughput\_loss} + 0.15\,\text{flow\_disruption}\),
  graded in \([0,1]\) with weights stated and consistency-checked on their own Saaty matrix, on the
  same footing as --- but separately from --- the quality-dimension weights of Section~\ref{sec:4.2}.
  \(I_{\text{comp}}\) backs the validation gates, the checks of Section~\ref{sec:5.4}, and
  remediation acceptance (Section~\ref{sec:6.1}).
\item
  \textbf{\(I_{\text{dyn}}(v)\)} --- produced by \texttt{MessageFlowSimulator}: the drop in delivered
  message rate that \emph{surviving} consumers experience, obtained not by traversing edges but by
  discrete-event simulation of actual traffic --- each publisher emits at its declared rate, every
  topic fans out into a bounded per-subscriber queue, and the fault is injected mid-run.
  \(I_{\text{dyn}}\) trains nothing and gates nothing: it is reported in Section~\ref{sec:7.4} as a
  construct-validity check on the other two.
\end{itemize}

\(I^*\) and \(I_{\text{comp}}\) agree only weakly --- mean Spearman \(\rho = 0.394\) across the seven
scenarios (Section~\ref{sec:7.4}). We therefore treat evidence gathered against one as \emph{not}
transferring to a claim measured against the other, and apply that constraint to our own analyses;
Section~\ref{sec:7.4} quantifies the agreement and label-coverage bounds, and Section~\ref{sec:8.2}
flags where the distinction bites. The two cascade oracles share a \texttt{propagation\_threshold}
(default 0.2) below which partial feed loss is treated as recoverable degradation rather than a
cascade trigger, and --- because intra-wave propagation order is tie-broken stochastically --- are run
over five seeds, with impact reported as the across-seed mean and standard deviation, the latter also
the noise scale the remediation filter of Section~\ref{sec:6.1} is calibrated against.

\hypertarget{two-predictors-over-the-same-model}{%
\subsection{Two Predictors over the Same Model}\label{two-predictors-over-the-same-model}}

\label{sec:5.2}

We evaluate two predictors of simulated cascade impact, deliberately spanning the
interpretability--capacity spectrum. The \textbf{interpretable predictor} is the composite \(Q(v)\)
of Section~\ref{sec:4}, computed deterministically on \(G_{\text{analysis}}\) with no learned
parameters. The \textbf{learned predictor} is a \textbf{Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT)}
\cite{hu2020hgt} that assigns relation-specific attention and message-passing parameters across the
five node types (App, Broker, Topic, Node, Library) and the structural/\texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} edge
types, so that message transformations differ by the semantic relation they traverse rather than being
shared across a flattened graph: each relation gets its own projection matrices inside the attention
computation, and the learned attention weight on an edge is directly interpretable as how much that
specific typed relation contributed to a neighbour's representation.

The \textbf{\(HGL\text{-}QoS\)} variant additionally injects the continuous QoS attributes into the
edge-attention aggregation, scaling message magnitude by interface contract strength; the base
\textbf{HGL} variant masks these fields to isolate the contribution of typing alone from that of QoS
encoding (RQ3, Section~\ref{sec:8.3}). Both variants consume features from \(G_{\text{analysis}}\)
(not the simulator) and are trained inductively against the \(I^*(v)\) labels of
Section~\ref{sec:5.1}. For RQ1 we report the two predictors separately rather than blended, so that
\emph{where} typed learning improves on the interpretable score is settled on like-for-like rankings.

\hypertarget{the-independence-guarantee}{%
\subsection{The Independence Guarantee}\label{the-independence-guarantee}}

\label{sec:5.3}

The evaluation is only meaningful if the predictor cannot see its own labels. Two structural
properties enforce this: predictors operate on \(G_{\text{analysis}}\) while both simulators operate
on \(G_{\text{structural}}\), so label-producing and feature-producing computations are distinct
passes over distinct graph views; and no simulation output is ever fed back as an input feature to
\(Q(v)\) or the learned predictor. For the learned predictor specifically, this is also checked at
inference time: the GNN service raises before a forward pass if a feature tensor carries a target
label attribute, a defensive check against a leakage bug introduced later in this codebase's lifetime
rather than a proof none exists today. The remediation stage (Section~\ref{sec:6}) obeys the same
discipline: its candidate-generation phase never reads simulated impact.

\hypertarget{two-checks-that-take-node-type-seriously}{%
\subsection{Two Checks That Take Node Type Seriously}\label{two-checks-that-take-node-type-seriously}}

\label{sec:5.4}

Two analyses probe whether the typed model's distinctions matter empirically, both measured against
\(I_{\text{comp}}(v)\) and both reported as negative or consistency results rather than as headline
findings.

\textbf{The shared-library blast mechanism.} Rule 5 (Section~\ref{sec:3.2}) gives libraries a
structurally distinctive \emph{simultaneous} failure mode, motivating the hypothesis that a library's
composite score \(Q(v)\) would understate its true cascade impact. Tested directly across all seven
scenarios (165 Library nodes), we did \textbf{not} find the hypothesized mismatch: the highest
composite score reached by any library is \(Q = 0.422\), and across every library in the corpus
\(I_{\text{comp}}(v)\) never exceeds \(Q(v)\) --- the composite score is, if anything, mildly
conservative for this node type. We report this as a negative result rather than omit it: the
mechanism itself remains a real structural distinction worth preserving in the model, and we retain
the FanOutReduction operator's blast-radius trigger (Section~\ref{sec:6.1}) as a structurally
motivated safeguard rather than as a mechanism validated by this particular result
(Section~\ref{sec:8.2} restates this alongside the measured edge-criticality finding it is adjacent
to but does not corroborate).

\textbf{Stratified correlation.} A single pooled correlation between \(Q(v)\) and simulated impact
could in principle mask a Simpson's-paradox-style effect if node types occupy sufficiently different
regions of the \((Q, I)\) plane. Pooling across all seven scenarios (1,545 nodes) gives
\(\rho = 0.374\); computed separately by node type, correlations range from 0.322 (Topic) to 0.429
(Broker), all significant at \(p < 0.01\). We do \textbf{not} find a Simpson's-paradox effect: the
pooled figure sits inside the per-type range rather than diverging from it, which is a useful
consistency result --- the predictive relationship holds at similar strength across every component
type --- even though the check happened not to overturn the pooled conclusion here. The effect it
looked for \emph{does} occur elsewhere in this study: pooling Application and Library nodes into a
single correlation moved HGL on \texttt{av\_system} from \(\rho = 0.836\) within Applications to
\(0.46\) pooled (Section~\ref{sec:8.2}), a case where a pooled figure was actively misleading and one
that went unnoticed until the evaluation contract of Section~\ref{sec:7.2} was imposed. Per D4
(Section~\ref{sec:4.1}), this pooled figure is a diagnostic over the union of seven within-system
rankings, not a cross-system criticality result, and no claim elsewhere in the paper rests on it.
