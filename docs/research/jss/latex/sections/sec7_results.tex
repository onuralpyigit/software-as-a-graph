\subsection{RQ1: SaG's Engines Against Structural Baselines}
\label{sec:rq1}
\label{sec:rq1-loso}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{On SaG's derived dependency graph, counting a component's dependents ranks its cascade impact as well as any engine in the study: \texttt{InDeg} reaches $\rho = 0.764$ and beats closed-form centrality (\texttt{Topo-QoS}, $0.553$) on all twelve held-out architectures ($+0.211$, Holm $p = 0.002$). Deriving the dependencies is what makes the count work: counting raw connections reaches $0.199$ ($+0.565$ for \texttt{InDeg}, 12/12). Graph neural networks that read the dependency graph reach the same level ($0.748$ and $0.758$). The hybrid engines significantly outperform closed-form centrality under their registered rules ($+0.103$ and $+0.130$, each on 11/12 folds, Holm $p \le 0.0068$).}

Each of the twelve folds holds out one scenario and trains on the remaining eleven, and every predictor is scored on the same Application node set (26 to 300 nodes, $K$ between 5 and 60). \noindent\textbf{Counting dependents on the derived graph ranks impact well.} \texttt{InDeg}, the number of components that depend on $v$ in the \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} graph, reaches $\rho = 0.764$ and beats \texttt{Topo-QoS} on every fold; \texttt{Reach}, the number of transitive dependents, reaches $0.732$ (11/12). Both were registered in Amendment~7 before their results existed. For an Application, \texttt{InDeg} is the number of distinct subscribers to the topics it publishes: publish--subscribe afferent coupling \cite{martin2003agile,rud2006ais,bogner2017maintainability}, which the derivation makes computable from an architecture model. Amendment~10 shows that the derivation is what makes the count predictive. Counting a component's raw connections in the multigraph scores $0.199$, and counting the topics it publishes $0.431$; \texttt{InDeg} beats both on all twelve folds ($+0.565$ and $+0.334$, Holm $p = 0.0015$). Transitive reach also profits from the derived library rule: without Rule~5, \texttt{Reach} falls by $0.058$ (9/12, Holm $p = 0.0068$).

\noindent\textbf{Learners that read the dependency graph match the count.} The same graph neural networks, trained on the dependency graph instead of the raw multigraph, reach $\rho = 0.748$ (\texttt{GAT-P-QoS}) and $0.758$ (Hybrid-GAT-P, which corrects the \texttt{InDeg} score). Both are statistically level with \texttt{InDeg} ($-0.017$ and $-0.006$, Holm $p \ge 0.88$) and neither exceeds it. Hybrid-GAT-P beats \texttt{Topo-QoS} on all twelve folds and has the highest Overlap@$K$ ($0.515$). Section~\ref{sec:rq2} shows why reading the dependency graph matters.

\noindent\textbf{Both registered hybrids significantly outperform closed-form centrality.} Hybrid-HGT reaches $\rho = 0.657$ ($+0.103$, Holm $p = 0.0068$) and Hybrid-GAT reaches $\rho = 0.683$ ($+0.130$, CI $[+0.075, +0.190]$, Holm $p = 0.0029$), each on 11 of 12 folds. Each meets the decision rule registered before its run, and both remain significant under one Holm correction pooled over all thirteen confirmatory contrasts of the study ($p_{\text{omni}} = 0.041$ and $0.019$; Section~\ref{sec:6.3}). On their own, the raw-multigraph learned engines are on par with \texttt{Topo-QoS} (\texttt{HGT-QoS} $+0.069$, $p = 0.266$; \texttt{GAT-QoS} $+0.082$, $p = 0.233$; registered GPU sweep $+0.085$, Holm $0.303$; Supplementary Section~\ref{S-supp:loso-gpu}).

\noindent\textbf{Why the hybrids work: the engines are complementary.} \texttt{HGT-QoS} loses to \texttt{Topo-QoS} on four folds, all where the closed-form engine is strongest: Real-Time Gaming, Enterprise ($0.426$ vs.\ $0.795$), AV System and Telecom RAN ($0.427$ vs.\ $0.576$). Its largest gains come where the closed-form engine is weakest: Healthcare, IoT Smart City, ATM and Microservices, $+0.210$ to $+0.362$ (Figure~\ref{fig:results}B). The prior removes the failure mode: on Enterprise, Hybrid-HGT and Hybrid-GAT reach $0.735$ and $0.768$, and Telecom RAN turns from a loss into a win for both. The cost is a smaller gain on the weakest folds.

\noindent\textbf{Where the closed-form gain comes from.} The registered comparator \texttt{Topo} reads betweenness from the analysis stage's application-layer graph, whereas \texttt{Topo-QoS} computes QoS-weighted betweenness on the Application--Library dependency graph. Three controls registered in Amendment~7 separate substrate from weighting (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:a7-controls}). Unweighted betweenness on the same graph scores $0.591$; fixing every topic weight to a constant scores $0.595$; permuting QoS profiles across topics scores $0.559$ ($-0.006$ against the declared profiles, $p = 0.73$). By decision rule R2 of Amendment~7, the $+0.204$ is therefore attributed to QoS-weighted dependency multiplicity on the Application--Library graph, not to the content of the declared contracts, and a QoS-independent control corpus shows the generator's QoS--topology coupling to be part of that mechanism (R2$'$). This matches the label analysis of Section~\ref{sec:4.3}, where the oracle's ordering is $96.5\%$ topological. Declared contracts pay off for learned engines instead (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}), and oracles that express deadline misses, durability replay or priority inversion are where their content can be expected to matter for closed-form ranking.

\noindent\textbf{Anchoring trades transfer for in-distribution accuracy.} On the five independently authored system models, both hybrids stay well above closed-form centrality ($0.695$ and $0.662$ vs.\ $0.511$--$0.526$) but below the pure learned engines ($0.760$ and $0.805$; Section~\ref{sec:rq3}). By the rule registered in Amendment~6, Hybrid-GAT therefore does not replace Hybrid-HGT as the recommended hybrid ($0.662 < 0.695$).

\noindent\textbf{Label noise and inert components.} Re-running the oracle across five seeds gives a test--retest rank correlation of $0.811$--$1.000$ (median $0.982$), well above every engine's mean $\rho$. Between $21\%$ and $52\%$ of each held-out population carries zero simulated impact. In the registered sweep, restricting evaluation to components with positive impact halves every predictor's correlation, learned or not, and leaves the method ordering unchanged (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-supp:loso-active}).

\subsubsection{How the Hybrids Are Built}
\label{sec:rq1-hybrid}

Each hybrid gives a learned engine the rank-normalized \texttt{Topo-QoS} score as one extra input per Application and Library, and adds a learned correction to that score on the logit scale, $\hat{I}^*(v) = \sigma\big(z(v) + \alpha\,\operatorname{logit}(p(v))\big)$, with one learnable $\alpha$ (Figure~\ref{fig:engines}a). Everything else matches the underlying engine. Hybrid-HGT is built on \texttt{HGT-QoS} (Amendment~5; $321$ extra parameters) and Hybrid-GAT on \texttt{GAT-QoS} (Amendment~6; $1{,}441$ extra parameters). Each was registered with its contrasts and decision rule before any run, with no setting tuned, and evaluated in its own CPU sweep with its comparators re-run in the same invocation.

\subsection{RQ2: What Learned Engines Need}
\label{sec:rq2}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{Learned engines need two things. The first is the dependency graph: the same networks gain $+0.075$ to $+0.113$ when they read it instead of the raw multigraph (11--12 of 12 folds, Holm $p \le 0.021$), because on it every component receives messages from its dependents. The second is the declared coupling inputs: with capacity and edge-channel width matched, QoS inputs raise learned ranking by $+0.073$ (10 of 12 folds), while relation-specific weights add nothing (typing main effect $-0.014$, interaction $+0.001$).}

\noindent\textbf{Learning pays off when it reads the dependency graph.} On the raw multigraph every relation points away from an Application, so the untyped networks score each Application from its own features, and HGT reaches it only through its reverse pass (Section~\ref{sec:8.2}). Amendment~9 moved the same learners, at the same parameter budgets and with bit-identical node features and labels, onto the \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} graph. There a 3-layer network's receptive field at an Application is exactly the Application and its dependents within three hops, on all twelve architectures. Table~\ref{tab:dg-learners} reports the registered contrasts. All three GAT arms beat their raw-multigraph counterparts, by $+0.090$ (\texttt{GAT-P}), $+0.113$ (\texttt{GAT-P-QoS}, 12/12) and $+0.075$ (Hybrid-GAT-P, 11/12), and the two QoS-reading arms reach the level of \texttt{InDeg} without exceeding it. Message passing now contributes beyond the node features: \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} beats the feature-only gradient-boosting control GBM-Feat by $+0.106$ (10/12, descriptive), where on the raw multigraph \texttt{GAT-QoS} was only level with it ($-0.007$). The typed \texttt{HGT-P-QoS} did not train stably on this graph (median within-fold seed spread $0.208$, against $0.026$ for \texttt{GAT-P-QoS}); its low mean reflects that failure rather than the graph, and it was reported as registered, untuned.

\noindent\textbf{QoS inputs are the working ingredient.} At matched capacity, adding the QoS inputs raises ranking by $+0.073$ with or without typing, on 10 of 12 folds each time, and significantly for the untyped pair ($+0.072$, CI $[+0.028, +0.109]$, $p = 0.016$). The QoS inputs also stabilize training: the median within-fold seed spread of the untyped pair falls from $0.083$ to $0.010$ (the small GATs and HGT show the same pattern; Supplementary Section~\ref{S-supp:loso-gpu}). The Q factor switches two inputs together: the 16-D edge vector and three node columns holding each component's declared coupling weights ($w$, $w_{\text{in}}$, $w_{\text{out}}$). An exploratory follow-up that separates them locates the gain in the node columns: removing them from \texttt{GAT-QoS} costs $0.095$ (11 of 12 folds, Holm $p = 0.024$), whereas adding the edge channel alone to \texttt{GAT} gives $-0.023$. The seed stabilization follows the same columns (median spread $0.010$ with them, $0.136$ without).

\noindent\textbf{Relation-specific weights add nothing once capacity is matched.} \texttt{HGT} and \texttt{HGT-QoS} are within $0.015$ of their untyped counterparts \texttt{GAT} and \texttt{GAT-QoS} and win only 4--5 of 12 folds against them. Because \texttt{GAT-QoS} receives each edge's relation type as a feature, the precise finding is that relation-typed \emph{parameters} add nothing beyond relation-typed \emph{inputs}. The two remaining registered controls agree. \texttt{GAT-w}, a capacity-matched untyped GAT that reads only the scalar weight $w(e)$, reaches $\rho = 0.633$, level with the 16-D \texttt{GAT-QoS}. \texttt{HGT-QoS-U}, which removes HGT's reverse pass and with it the only route by which messages reach an Application, reaches $0.632$ against \texttt{HGT-QoS}'s $0.622$ (registered contrast \texttt{HGT-QoS} vs.\ \texttt{HGT-QoS-U} $-0.010$, \texttt{HGT-QoS} ahead on 6/12 folds, $p = 0.91$; Section~\ref{sec:8.2}).

\noindent\textbf{How much QoS the target can reward.} $I^*(v)$ is a near-topological target: a topology-only relabeling recovers its ordering at mean $\rho = 0.965$, and QoS acts mainly at its top-$K$ boundary (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}). On this target the QoS inputs therefore act largely as a coupling-strength signal. Oracles that express deadline misses, durability replay or priority inversion would let the encodings contribute contract semantics as well.

\subsection{RQ3: Zero-Shot Transfer to Models of Open-Source Systems}
\label{sec:rq3}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{The dependency structure SaG derives transfers without training: on independently authored models of five open-source systems, counting transitive dependents ranks impact at $\rho = 0.938$ $[0.879, 0.991]$ and counting direct dependents at $0.863$, and transitive reach keeps $\rho = 0.871$ on the components that actually propagate failures. Learned engines trained only on synthetic scenarios also transfer, far above closed-form centrality: \texttt{HGT-QoS} reaches $0.760$ $[0.714, 0.819]$ and \texttt{GAT-QoS} $0.805$ $[0.759, 0.868]$, against $0.511$--$0.526$, and they nearly double its top-$K$ critical-set overlap.}

The five systems are hand-authored models of Autoware.universe (ROS~2), EdgeX Foundry and Home Assistant, plus meshes modelled after Online Boutique and Train-Ticket (Section~\ref{sec:6.1}). They were written independently of the scenario generator but are models rather than extractions, and they carry labels from the same oracles. The test is therefore transfer to independently authored topologies under simulated reachability. \texttt{HGT-QoS} and \texttt{GAT-QoS} were trained on all twelve synthetic scenarios and evaluated zero-shot at the same 3-layer, 300-epoch budget as every other learned result. No system model contributed gradients or checkpoint selection. Where a system declares no QoS manifest, standard middleware defaults (ROS~2 Best-Effort/Reliable, MQTT QoS 0/1) are applied uniformly across all predictors.

\noindent\textbf{Dependency structure transfers without training.} The dependency counts need no training data, so their transfer is immediate. \texttt{Reach} reaches $\rho = 0.938$ on the five system models ($0.836$--$0.998$ per system) and \texttt{InDeg} $0.863$ (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid}; Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:a7-systems}). These systems are small and have simple library structure: none of them has a Library that publishes or subscribes, so Rule-1-only reach equals \texttt{Reach} on every one of them (Amendment~10), and the counts separate inert from active components almost perfectly. They are therefore an easier ranking target than the synthetic folds, for every predictor.

\noindent\textbf{Learned engines transfer far above closed-form centrality, independently of typing.} Both learned engines lead closed-form centrality on 4 of 5 systems, with intervals that do not overlap its intervals (Table~\ref{tab:hybrid}). The untyped \texttt{GAT-QoS} beats \texttt{HGT-QoS} on all five systems ($\rho = 0.805$ vs.\ $0.760$). It also scores higher on Overlap@$K$ ($0.519$ vs.\ $0.470$) and PR-AUC ($0.790$ vs.\ $0.713$). Consistent with RQ2, what transfers is learning over SaG's QoS-annotated graph, not relation-specific parameters. Identification separates the engines most clearly: top-$K$ overlap averages $0.470$ against $0.248$ for the closed-form scores, and PR-AUC $0.713$ against $0.474$--$0.521$. On EdgeX, symmetric adapter-to-broker stars create betweenness ties that collapse closed-form triage entirely (Overlap@$K = 0.000$).

\noindent\textbf{Active components.} Restricted to components that propagate failures, \texttt{HGT-QoS} keeps a positive mean correlation ($\rho_{>0} = +0.236$) where closed-form centrality turns negative, and transitive reach keeps $\rho_{>0} = 0.871$ (\texttt{InDeg} $0.321$). Every learned interval spans zero at five systems, so the learned-versus-centrality comparison on this stratum is unresolved. Learners trained on the dependency graph (Amendment~9) transfer at $0.792$--$0.830$, level with their raw-multigraph counterparts (Table~\ref{tab:dg-learners}). $\rho_{>0}$ is positive on the three models of publish--subscribe systems and non-positive on the two modelled after RPC systems. Because both RPC-derived models are encoded as publish--subscribe graphs, this split cannot be attributed to call-tree semantics (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). An earlier, non-blind 2-layer configuration leaves every conclusion unchanged (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-supp:taxonomy}).

\subsection{RQ4: Analysis Cost}
\label{sec:rq4}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{Neural inference is effectively free ($56\,\text{ms}$ for a 2{,}000-node architecture, $0.02\%$ of pipeline time). Cost is dominated by deterministic feature extraction, specifically the $O(|V|^2 + |V||E|)$ Connectivity Degradation Index, and it tracks the density of the derived dependency projection. Cold extraction takes $2$--$18\times$ (median $5.6\times$) as long as the in-process cascade simulation, so SaG's advantage for the learned engines is avoiding staging infrastructure rather than saving CPU time. The strongest rankers are also the cheapest: deriving the dependency graph and counting dependents takes at most $0.12\,\text{s}$ per scenario, including the 300-application Enterprise architecture, and needs none of the feature extraction.}

At 2,000 components the HGT forward pass takes $56\,\text{ms}$ against $239\,\text{s}$ for structural analysis (Table~\ref{tab:scale}). Because the analysis stage produces the node features the forward pass consumes, end-to-end evaluation of an unseen 2,000-component architecture takes about four minutes, of which the learned model is $0.02\%$. The $56\,\text{ms}$ is the marginal cost of re-scoring an already-analysed graph. The dominant term is the Connectivity Degradation Index, computed for every node in the main connected component. That is a correctness requirement: restricting CDI to articulation points leaves it identically zero in redundant multi-publisher topologies and makes $A(v)$ near-constant.

Across the corpus, the complete analysis gate (structural analysis plus 18 anti-pattern detectors) runs in $0.16$--$79.3\,\text{s}$ per scenario. That is $2.0$--$17.7\times$ (median $5.6\times$) the five-seed cascade labeling sweep measured in the same session, and the gate is more expensive on all twelve scenarios. The premium tracks the size of the derived projection rather than component count; Enterprise, with 300 applications on 120 topics, is the maximum (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-supp:gate-ratio}). On raw CPU time, direct simulation is therefore faster wherever its parameters are available. SaG additionally scores infrastructure components and dependency edges and avoids staging infrastructure. Training the four learned arms once took $7.7$ CPU-hours, amortized over every later evaluation.
