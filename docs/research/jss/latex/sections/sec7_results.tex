\subsection{RQ1: Ranking Accuracy}
\label{sec:rq1}
\label{sec:rq1-hybrid}
\label{sec:rq1-loso}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{For the reachability and queue-flow oracles, explicit dependency graphs, rather than model complexity, carry most of the ranking signal. The strongest learned model, the graph attention network on the dependency graph (\texttt{GAT-P-QoS}, $\rho = 0.748$), approaches without exceeding the direct-dependent reference ($0.764$; not distinguishable, equivalence not established). On the expensive queue-flow oracle, a gradient-boosted approximation trained on its labels reaches $0.799$ because it reads declared publication rates and payload sizes; the rate-weighted reference, which reads the same rates without training, reaches $0.830$ and exceeds the learned approximation ($+0.031$, 10 of 12 folds; exploratory), and learners that start from the reference do not improve on it. On the multi-criteria oracle, training-free scores aligned with its terms rank highest and pure neural models drop sharply. The registered primary comparison on the raw multigraph (\texttt{HGT-QoS} vs.\ \texttt{Topo-QoS}) was null ($\Delta\rho = +0.069$, $p = 0.266$).}

Learned rankers are trained on eleven scenarios and scored on the twelfth; training-free rankers are scored on all twelve (26 to 300 Applications, $K$ between 5 and 60). Table~\ref{tab:hybrid} reports all of them against $I^*$, with the reference rankings of Section~\ref{sec:6.2} in a separate block: they restate $I^*$'s rule, so their rows measure the circularity and carry no contrast.

Two patterns emerge. First, reading the dependency graph instead of the raw multigraph improves attention-based learners (\texttt{GAT-P-QoS} $0.748$ vs.\ \texttt{GAT-QoS} $0.635$), although the typed transformer becomes unstable on it (\texttt{HGT-P-QoS} $0.514$). Second, analytical rankings derived from the same dependency structure are not exceeded by any learned model ($0.764$--$0.808$, against at most $0.748$). The analyses below examine both observations. The ordering depends on how folds are weighted: weighting by $|V_{\text{app}}|$ keeps the top five rows but lifts \texttt{Topo-QoS} to $0.596$, level with the raw-multigraph learned rankers ($0.595$--$0.606$).

\noindent\textbf{The reference level.} \texttt{InDeg} is publish--subscribe afferent coupling \cite{martin2003agile,rud2006ais,bogner2017maintainability} and counts exactly the Applications reached by $I^*$'s first wave; \texttt{Reach} counts the transitive dependents a reachability cascade can visit. Their values ($0.764$, $\rho_{>0} = 0.516$; $0.732$) measure how much of the oracle restating its rule recovers, not predictive skill. The derivation matters for one of them: without Rule~5, \texttt{Reach} drops by $0.058$ (9/12 folds, Holm $p = 0.0068$). Untyped raw-graph metrics do not recover the reference (total degree $0.199$, reverse PageRank $0.089$; PageRank is constant over Applications, which have no incoming raw edges).

\noindent\textbf{The first-order expansion of the oracle.} The strongest reference, the closed-form first-order expansion of $I^*$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:analytic_istar}), reaches $0.808$ $[0.755, 0.853]$ ($\rho_{>0} = 0.631$): direct subscriber loss is most of what $I^*$ measures, and \texttt{InDeg} is a coarser version of it (it slightly exceeds the expansion on four folds).

\noindent\textbf{Learners on the dependency graph approach the reference.} \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} reaches $\rho = 0.748$, $0.016$ below the direct-dependent count per seed and $0.007$ above it as a five-seed ensemble ($0.772$, Table~\ref{tab:independent_oracles}). Neither difference is significant, and neither shows equivalence: the $90\%$ interval of the per-fold difference is $\pm 0.076$, so a two one-sided test at the registered margin of $\pm 0.05$ does not pass ($p = 0.17$ per seed, $0.13$ for the ensemble). The closeness depends on the degree features (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). \texttt{HGT-P-QoS} reaches only $0.514$ and is highly seed-dependent (Section~\ref{sec:8.3}).

\noindent\textbf{Hybrid rankers versus the training-free baseline and their base learners.} A hybrid takes $p(v)$ as an input and corrects its logit, so it nests its comparator. Hybrid-HGT ($0.657$, $+0.103$, Holm $p = 0.0068$) and Hybrid-GAT ($0.683$, $+0.130$, Holm $p = 0.0029$) beat \texttt{Topo-QoS} on 11 of 12 folds, also under the omnibus Holm correction ($p_{\text{omni}} = 0.041$ and $0.019$), the Nadeau--Bengio corrected $t$ ($p = 0.019$ and $0.014$) and $|V_{\text{app}}|$ weighting (sign-flip $p = 0.038$ and $0.005$). Neither differs from its own base learner (Hybrid-HGT vs.\ \texttt{HGT-QoS} $+0.035$, $p = 0.73$; Hybrid-GAT vs.\ \texttt{GAT-QoS} $+0.048$, $p = 0.30$). Retrained with the articulation defect corrected (Table~\ref{tab:a16}), Hybrid-GAT-AP reaches $0.669$ ($+0.136$, Holm $p = 0.0059$) and Hybrid-HGT-AP $0.640$ ($+0.107$, Holm $p = 0.0073$), each on 11 of 12 folds, and again neither differs from its base learner ($+0.034$ and $+0.018$, Holm $p = 0.94$). The hybrid gain therefore belongs to the weak comparator, not to the learned correction. Given the direct-dependent count as their prior, \texttt{GAT-QoS+InDeg} ($0.763$) and \texttt{HGT-QoS+InDeg} ($0.759$) gain $+0.128$ and $+0.137$ over their base learners (Holm $p = 0.0049$) and land within $\pm 0.012$ of \texttt{InDeg} itself (equivalent at $\pm 0.05$, TOST $p < 0.001$): a learner handed the reference reproduces it. The prior also hurts transfer (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).

\noindent\textbf{Beyond the reachability oracle.} Table~\ref{tab:independent_oracles} compares the rankers against two other oracles. On the full-population $I_{\text{dyn}}$, which agrees with $I^*$ at $\rho = 0.711$ $[0.625, 0.794]$, the references outperform all GNNs (first-order expansion $0.706$, \texttt{InDeg} $0.664$, GNNs $0.496$--$0.615$). Because $I_{\text{dyn}}$ shares its topology and first-order effect with $I^*$, partial correlations show what each ranking keeps beyond them. After removing the rank of $I^*$, \texttt{InDeg} keeps $0.272$ $[0.188, 0.366]$, \texttt{Reach} $0.117$, \texttt{Topo-QoS} $0.134$ and the GNNs $0.107$--$0.173$, all intervals excluding zero; after removing the first-order expansion instead, \texttt{InDeg} keeps $0.079$ and the raw-multigraph GNNs $0.212$--$0.217$, so the learners capture some queue-flow signal beyond the unweighted first-order term, though less than the rate-weighted reference ($0.573$).

The queue-flow oracle separates what the dependency structure provides from what declared rates add. Weighting each term of the first-order expansion (Eq.~\eqref{eq:analytic_istar}, $0.706$ on $I_{\text{dyn}}$) by the topic's declared publication rate (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rate-expansion}) raises it to $0.830$ $[0.778, 0.872]$ without training (exploratory). A gradient-boosted model trained on $I_{\text{dyn}}$ labels, with counts, the first-order expansion and a feature block that carries declared rates and payload sizes, reaches $0.799$: above the unweighted expansion ($+0.094$, Holm $p = 0.019$) but falls below the rate-weighted reference, which exceeds it by $+0.031$ $[+0.013, +0.049]$ on 10 of 12 folds (nominal Holm $p = 0.009$; exploratory). An exploratory attribution (Supplementary Section~\ref{supp:advisor-v6}) shows where its gain comes from: the declared rate and payload columns alone add $+0.097$ (9/12 folds, Holm $p = 0.021$), and the seven QoS-derived columns ($w(t)$-weighted scores and policy shares) add $-0.005$ ($p = 0.68$). The GNN trained on the same labels, which received neither rates nor payloads, reaches $0.598$, below the first-order expansion ($-0.108$, Holm $p = 0.014$) and no better than the same GNN trained on $I^*$ labels restricted to Applications, a label-support control run in the same sweep ($-0.008$).
\noindent\textbf{Learning on top of the rate-weighted reference.} The direct test of whether learning adds value beyond the aligned approximation is a learner that starts from it (Table~\ref{tab:a17}, F12). Given Eq.~\eqref{eq:rate-expansion} as one more input column, the gradient-boosted approximation reaches $0.830$, the formula's value ($+0.000$, 5 of 12 folds, Holm $p = 0.97$); trained to correct the formula's residual, it reaches $0.824$ ($-0.006$, Holm $p = 0.68$); and the GAT trained on $I_{\text{dyn}}$ with the formula as its prior gains $+0.214$ over the same GAT without it (12 of 12 folds) but still falls below the formula ($0.812$, $-0.018$, 1 of 12 folds, Holm $p = 0.0029$). No learner improved on the rate-weighted reference; on this simulator, the learned approximations recover the formula's information and add none.

\noindent\textbf{The multi-criteria oracle favors different rankers.} On $I_{\text{comp}}$, learned rankers without a prior perform poorly ($0.144$--$0.334$; sum-aggregation GNNs $0.41$--$0.44$), the hybrids keep $0.58$, and training-free scores aligned with its fragmentation and flow terms rank highest: \texttt{Topo-QoS} $0.702$ $[0.649, 0.753]$ (above \texttt{InDeg}, $0.650$, which is ahead on only 3 of 12 folds, $p = 0.110$) and raw total degree $0.719$. As with the references on $I^*$, this reflects overlap with the oracle's terms, not predictive skill. No ranker is best across all three oracles.

\noindent\textbf{Identifying the critical set.} In a CI gate the question is whether the true top-ranked items are flagged. Figure~\ref{fig:recall} shows the share of the true top-20\% set on $I^*$ that each ranker places in its top $k\%$, with ties resolved by averaging. At $k = 20\%$, \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} recovers $0.48$ of the critical set and \texttt{Topo-QoS} recovers $0.38$, matching their Overlap@$K$; about half of what a top-20\% gate flags is outside the true top 20\%. To recover 80\% of the critical set, \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} must flag the top 40\% ($0.81$), and \texttt{Topo-QoS} does not reach 80\% within 50\%. The references do only slightly better: at $k = 20\%$, \texttt{InDeg} recovers $0.49$ (tie-breaking bounds $0.48$--$0.50$), and 80\% recall requires the top 45\% by \texttt{InDeg} ($0.83$) or by the first-order expansion ($0.89$); only the latter reaches 90\% within the top 50\%. On the exhaustive $I_{\text{dyn}}$ labels (panel~B), \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} reaches $0.74$ at 40\% and $0.82$ at 50\%, compared to $0.80$ at 40\% for the \texttt{InDeg} reference; the rate-weighted reference, which restates $I_{\text{dyn}}$'s first-order rule, recovers $0.81$ of that set at 20\% and $0.96$ at 30\% (exploratory). Flagging 40--50\% of components to catch 80\% of the critical ones would cause alert fatigue, so SaG suits a prioritized triage list for architecture review rather than a blocking gate (Section~\ref{sec:8.1}).

\subsection{RQ2: Sources of Predictive Performance}
\label{sec:rq2}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{Attention-based learners improve on the dependency graph, where messages reach the scored Applications from their dependents ($0.748$ vs.\ $0.635$ on the raw multigraph), and the gain survives two controls: a raw-graph GAT that passes every edge in both directions ($+0.072$ over it), and the removal of every oracle-aligned feature ($+0.231$ over the equally stripped reverse-edge control). Its closeness to the direct-dependent count comes from its degree features ($-0.136$ without them), whereas a sum-aggregation GNN, which can count, needs none: with no node features at all it reaches $0.719$. QoS inputs add nothing measurable once weighted in-degree is held, and the typing comparison is uninformative because it ran where messages are inert. Node order before training moves the learned values by about $0.04$ (three permutations average $0.729$ for \texttt{GAT-P-QoS}), a source of variance rather than a favourable published order.}

\noindent\textbf{The ``QoS'' factor was mostly the weighted in-degree.} In the capacity-matched $2\times2$ on the raw multigraph (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:contrasts_matched}; cell means $0.563$, $0.548$, $0.635$, $0.622$), relation typing has no effect ($-0.014$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.940$) and the ``QoS'' inputs add $+0.073$ ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.127$). The QoS-off arms, however, also zero $w_{\text{in}}$, a QoS-weighted version of \texttt{InDeg}. With $w_{\text{in}}$ held in both arms (Table~\ref{tab:a14}, F4), the ``QoS'' effect drops to $+0.030$ $[-0.023, +0.085]$ (Holm $p = 0.33$), so more than half of it was the weighted in-degree. Closed-form controls agree: unweighted betweenness ($0.591$) and constant topic weights ($0.595$) match or exceed QoS-weighted betweenness ($0.553$), and permuting QoS profiles across topics has no effect ($\Delta = -0.006$, $p = 0.733$). Because messages never reach Applications on the raw multigraph, this $2\times2$ compares per-node models and says nothing about relational typing (Section~\ref{sec:8.2}).

\noindent\textbf{What the dependency-graph learner needs.} Each learner receives in-degree and $w_{\text{in}}$ as node properties and, on the dependency graph, also aggregates its dependents, so its closeness to \texttt{InDeg} could come from either. Removing the two degree columns lowers \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} by $0.136$, and also removing the three centralities most correlated with in-degree by $0.161$ (Table~\ref{tab:a14}, F1). Theory predicts this: softmax attention averages over neighbors and cannot count them, whereas sum aggregation can \cite{xu2019gin,corso2020pna}, and a GINE network of the same size keeps $0.721$ and $0.711$ without the same columns. These aggregator contrasts are not significant after Holm correction ($+0.108$ and $+0.124$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.157$); with every oracle-aligned feature removed, the corresponding contrast is (below). In zero-shot, the degree columns matter little ($0.810$--$0.821$ without them), because the system models are ranked mostly by separating inert from active components.

\noindent\textbf{Direction versus dependency semantics.} On the raw multigraph a forward GAT never delivers a message to an Application (Section~\ref{sec:4.1}), so the $+0.113$ gain of \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} over \texttt{GAT-QoS} could come from edge direction rather than from dependency semantics. A control passes every raw-graph edge in both directions, with shared weights and an unchanged parameter count (\texttt{GAT-QoS-R}; Table~\ref{tab:a16}). It reaches $0.676$: direction alone recovers $+0.041$ of the gain, which is not significant (Holm $p = 0.064$), and \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} still exceeds it by $+0.072$ $[+0.034, +0.113]$ on 10 of 12 folds (Holm $p = 0.014$). The derived dependency graph therefore adds beyond edge direction. Reverse edges also cost transfer: \texttt{GAT-QoS-R} reaches $0.744$ zero-shot, against $0.805$ for \texttt{GAT-QoS} and $0.806$ for \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} (Table~\ref{tab:system_models_transfer}).

\noindent\textbf{Without the oracle-aligned features.} Every published learner reads features that compute part of what $I^*$ computes (Section~\ref{sec:3.5}). Zeroing all of them (in-degree, $w_{\text{in}}$, reverse PageRank, the articulation score, multi-path coupling, fan-out criticality and CDI; Table~\ref{tab:a17}, F11) tests whether the representation gain is carried by those features. It is not. The raw-graph learners collapse without them (\texttt{GAT-QoS} to $0.369$, the reverse-edge control to $0.378$), whereas the dependency-graph GAT keeps $0.610$ and beats the reverse-edge control by $+0.231$ (10 of 12 folds, Holm $p = 0.0068$). The oracle-aligned set costs \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} $0.138$, about what the two degree columns alone cost ($0.136$), so the remaining features add little. With them removed, sum aggregation beats attention on the dependency graph ($+0.115$, 9 of 12 folds, Holm $p = 0.027$): once the count is not supplied as a feature, the aggregator that can count matters. Without any node features at all, a sum-aggregation GNN on the dependency graph reaches $0.719$, $0.045$ below \texttt{InDeg} (1 of 12 folds): from structure alone it learns most of the dependent count, which the derived graph makes available and the raw multigraph does not.

\noindent\textbf{Node order.} ListMLE breaks label ties in input order, which is the generator's creation order (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). Node order is therefore a source of variance of about $0.04$ for the learned rankers, not evidence of a favourable order. Creation order correlates only weakly with the labels ($\rho = -0.20$ to $+0.29$ per fold), and each permutation also changes which nodes the seeded validation split draws, so the spread mixes tie order and validation draw. Learned-ranker differences smaller than this spread, such as the $-0.016$ between \texttt{GIN-P-QoS} and \texttt{GAT-P-QoS}, should not be read as differences between models.

\noindent\textbf{Message passing on the raw multigraph, and the selection rule.} On the raw multigraph no message reaches an Application: a gradient-boosted model on the same per-node features and no graph (\texttt{GBM-Feat}) reaches $0.642$, level with \texttt{GAT-QoS}, and removing HGT's reverse pass, its only route to Applications, has no effect (\texttt{HGT-QoS-U}, $-0.010$, $p = 0.91$). On the dependency graph, \texttt{HGT-P-QoS} ($0.514$) was unstable across seeds in its single untuned configuration (Section~\ref{sec:8.2}). The plan's nested hyperparameter selection, applied later on the stage-1 grid, it changes \texttt{HGT-QoS} from $0.622$ to $0.677$ ($+0.055$) and \texttt{GAT-P-QoS} from $0.748$ to $0.693$ ($-0.054$), neither significant (Holm $p = 0.259$); nested \texttt{HGT-QoS} versus \texttt{Topo-QoS} is $+0.123$ on 9 of 12 folds, still not significant (Holm $p = 0.192$). That harness trains on one scenario fewer and stops early on a held-out scenario, and on the three folds where it chose the published hyperparameters it does not reproduce the published scores (differences up to $0.10$), so it compares two protocols, not only two configurations (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}).

\subsection{RQ3: Zero-Shot Transfer to Models of Open-Source Systems}
\label{sec:rq3}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{On five hand-crafted models of open-source systems, the best learned rankers achieve about $0.81$ zero-shot ranking for $I^*$ (\texttt{GAT-P-QoS} $0.806$, \texttt{GAT-QoS} $0.805$), much higher than the training-free baseline ($0.526$). However, their agreement on the active stratum is weak ($\rho_{>0} \le 0.342$). The reference rankings are even higher (transitive dependents $0.938$, $\rho_{>0} = 0.871$; direct dependents $0.863$), since in these models, most of $I^*$ is just reachability. These models are structurally dissimilar; the first author wrote all five, so these results reflect transfer to these models, not to real-world systems. The baseline prior reduces transfer: both hybrids perform worse than their base learners ($0.695$ and $0.662$ compared to $0.760$ and $0.805$), and correcting the prior's defect does not change this ($0.702$ and $0.668$; Table~\ref{tab:a16}).}

\subsection{RQ4: Cost}
\label{sec:rq4}

\paragraph{Summary} \emph{Measured like for like, on the same graphs in one session (Table~\ref{tab:cost-ll}), one labeling pass of the reachability oracle over the Applications takes $0.01$--$0.72$~s per corpus architecture, the counting path that restates its first wave $17$--$176\times$ cheaper (median $45\times$), and the feature extraction every learned ranker needs $4.5$--$72.5\times$ more (median $16.9\times$). On generated graphs of up to $5{,}000$ components, the ordering holds, with features at $5.7$--$18.7\times$ one pass. The queue-flow oracle is the expensive one: $12.7$ CPU-hours to label the corpus. Estimated from the processor's base power ($28$~W TDP), replacing the dynamic simulation with the rate-weighted reference (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rate-expansion}), computed in at most about a millisecond per architecture, avoids an estimated $355.6$~Wh for the corpus. For reachability, static counting remains the greenest choice. Cost should be read together with ranking quality: for both $I^*$ and $I_{\text{dyn}}$, no learned alternative exceeds the analytical approximations, which avoid training and run in milliseconds.}

Table~\ref{tab:cost-ll} times each stage on the same graphs in one session, so the ratios compare like with like; the full detection gate and the five-seed labeling sweep are listed for reference, since no ranker needs them. The counting path is the cheapest, one $I^*$ pass over the scored population is next, and the app-layer feature extraction that the learned rankers read is the most expensive. Its articulation and Connectivity Degradation Index phase accounts for $88$--$91\%$ of it (Enterprise, and the $2{,}000$-component graph); this cost belongs to the chosen feature set, not to learned ranking as such, and a learner restricted to cheaper features would avoid it at an accuracy this study did not measure. Where the $I^*$ ranking itself is wanted, running the oracle is therefore cheaper than approximating it with a learned ranker at every size measured, and the count is cheaper still because it restates the oracle's first wave. Inference itself is negligible (tens of milliseconds at $2{,}000$ components), the count scales to $10{,}000$ components ($1.1$~s) and \texttt{Reach}, a transitive closure, less well ($23$~s); timing artifacts are in \path{docs/research/jss/experiments/rq4-cost.md}. The expensive oracle is $I_{\text{dyn}}$: labeling the corpus took $12.7$ CPU-hours (about $7$~s per fault run and seed; from $0.01$ CPU-hours for Healthcare to $5.88$ for Financial Trading), and although fault runs parallelize, a seed-parallel run is bounded by its slowest seed (about $76$~min). The rate-weighted reference approximates it in at most about a millisecond per architecture. Hardware power counters were not available to us (RAPL requires elevated privileges on the measurement machine), so energy is estimated as wall-clock time multiplied by the processor's base power ($28$~W TDP, Intel Core i7-1370P; \path{reproduce/energy_estimate.py}). Under this estimate, labeling the corpus with $I_{\text{dyn}}$ took about $355.6$~Wh, the five-seed $I^*$ labeling sweep $11.1$~s ($0.086$~Wh), and the full detection gate $106.9$~s ($0.83$~Wh). Summed over the twelve folds, the feature extraction that the learned rankers read took $70.0$~s (about $0.54$~Wh), and the dependency count $0.037$~s (about $1$~J). A learned approximation could repay its cost only by displacing repeated runs of an expensive simulator such as $I_{\text{dyn}}$, and here the rate-weighted reference already does so without training \cite{calero2015green,verdecchia2023greenai,schwartz2020greenai}.
