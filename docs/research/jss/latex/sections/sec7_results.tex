Per-fold evaluated populations range from 26 to 300 Application nodes, so $K = \text{round}(0.20\,|V_{\text{app}}|)$ ranges from 5 to 60. On $F_1@K$, HGL-QoS beats Topo-QoS in 4 of 8 folds ($W = 9.0$, $p = 0.469$) and GL in 8 of 8 ($W = 0.0$, $p = 0.0078$): the learned model separates itself from untyped learning on this metric, but not from the training-free QoS baseline.

\noindent\textbf{Key Insights for RQ1:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The typed predictor is the best learned configuration overall.} HGL-QoS leads every other learned variant on both metrics ($\rho = 0.608$, $F_1@K = 0.414$), with a significant ranking margin over both homogeneous GNNs ($+0.246$ over GL-QoS and $+0.522$ over GL, 8/8 folds, $p = 0.0078$ in both cases). Section~\ref{sec:7.2} states the substrate caveat that qualifies this comparison.
    \item \textbf{Critical-set identification separates learned models from each other, but not from the untrained QoS baseline.} HGL-QoS improves $F_1@K$ over GL by $+0.177$. Against Topo-QoS the margin is only $+0.034$ ($0.414$ vs.\ $0.380$) and does not survive testing: HGL-QoS wins 4 of 8 folds, and the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test over folds gives $W = 9.0$, $p = 0.469$. Per-fold differences alternate in sign ($+0.000$, $+0.188$, $-0.087$, $+0.067$, $+0.120$, $-0.014$, $+0.020$, $-0.022$). Critical-set identification is therefore not a demonstrated advantage of the learned model over the QoS-weighted baseline on this corpus.
    \item \textbf{QoS encoding carries the typed model out of distribution.} HGL-QoS leads HGL by $\Delta\rho = +0.169$, winning 7 of 8 folds ($p = 0.0156$) --- a substantial, consistent advantage under distribution shift.
    \item \textbf{Ranking Boundary:} Against \emph{Topo-QoS}, HGL-QoS's margin is $+0.037$, won in 5 of 8 folds ($p = 0.64$, not statistically significant). On out-of-distribution ranking, heterogeneous graph learning matches rather than outperforms a well-constructed QoS baseline, and the critical-set margin above is likewise not significant. The case for training a model therefore rests on the qualitative capabilities the baseline cannot supply --- actionable explanations, per-relationship edge criticality, and multi-task quality attributions --- which this study describes but does not yet quantify (Section~\ref{sec:8.4}).
    \item \textbf{The explanation layer is weakly predictive, not noise.} RM/$Q(v)$ achieves $\rho = 0.195$ under distribution shift --- outperforming untyped GL ($0.086$) while providing interpretable architectural diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:5}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{RQ2: Value of Typed Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:7.2}

To evaluate the specific contribution of node and edge typing, we contrast relation-specific HGL against homogeneous GL:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In-Distribution:} Heterogeneous HGL leads homogeneous GL by $\Delta\rho = +0.114$ ($0.725$ vs.\ $0.611$; $p = 0.375$, 5/7 scenarios won). On familiar topologies, homogeneous GNNs partially approximate typing via structural degree signatures.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Distribution (LOSO):} The gap widens sharply under distribution shift. Heterogeneous HGL outperforms homogeneous GL by \textbf{$+0.353$} ($\rho = 0.439$ vs.\ $0.086$), winning \emph{all eight} folds (paired Wilcoxon, $p = 0.0078$). Similarly, HGL-QoS outperforms GL-QoS by $+0.246$ ($0.608$ vs.\ $0.363$, $p = 0.0078$). Without relation typing, homogeneous models degrade below unweighted structural baselines ($0.086$ vs.\ $0.301$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Scope Condition and Confound Dissection.} As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:6.2.substrate}, GL and HGL are evaluated on distinct graph views: GL/GL-QoS operate on the Application--Library \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} projection (preventing structural collapse on raw graphs), whereas HGL/HGL-QoS ingest the complete native multigraph. Consequently, HGL's out-of-distribution margin reflects both relation-specific attention parameterization and direct topological visibility into the multi-entity infrastructure layer (brokers, topics, and host nodes). We therefore report this result as a comparison between typed learning on the full multigraph and untyped learning on a projected view, not as an isolated measurement of typing. The control that would isolate typing --- an untyped architecture over the identical multigraph with node-type indicators supplied as input features --- is not run here and is the principal outstanding validation of our central claim.

\subsubsection{Empirical Edge-Removal Analysis}

We further evaluated edge criticality by simulating the removal of candidate communication channels while keeping endpoint components operational. Across 50 candidate bridge edges in the \texttt{av\_system} topology, 46 edges produced zero downstream cascade impact, while the 4 edges with non-zero impact were direct communication channels (\texttt{PUBLISHES\_TO} / \texttt{SUBSCRIBES\_TO}). This confirms that individual communication links are largely redundant, whereas component outages and shared library dependencies induce disproportionate systemic disruption.

\subsubsection{Domain-Specific Weighting and Threshold Sensitivity}

Sweeping composite reliability weight $w_R \in [0, 1]$ moves mean $\rho$ by only $0.024$ ($0.341 \to 0.365$), with mean Kendall correlation $\tau = 0.974$ between domain and static rankings. \subsubsection{Availability Metric Recalibration}
\label{sec:7.3.availability}

In initial formulations, hard articulation-point gating caused Availability $A(v)$ to collapse to a constant multiple of $w(v)$ in six of eight scenarios (the pre-recalibration formulation weighted $w(v)$ at $0.05$; the shipped formulation of Equation~(\ref{eq:availability}) weights it at $0.0878$) where applications do not form cut vertices. Replacing the hard cut-vertex gate with a continuous redundancy-deficit metric (fractional increase in average shortest paths upon removal) widened $\sigma_A$ to $[0.0254, 0.0530]$ and restored structural discriminability.

\subsubsection{Anti-Pattern Detection and Node-Type Stratification}

Validating our rule-based anti-pattern catalog against $I_{\text{comp}}(v)$ yielded mean precision $0.239$ and recall $0.900$ ($F_1 = 0.3781$), reflecting a high-recall CI/CD safety posture. Path-enumeration detector \texttt{DEEP\_PIPELINE} timed out on larger graphs, identifying a known scalability limitation of exhaustive search.

\noindent\textbf{Stratification vs. Pooling:} Stratified RM rank correlations are $\rho = 0.503$ (Application), $0.395$ (Broker), and $0.142$ (Node), while pooled correlation across all types is $\rho = 0.028$. Pooling node types triggers Simpson's paradox by mixing distinct base rates. Classifying components within node types changes the criticality tier of $62.8\%$ of components (with $19.0\%$ crossing the critical boundary), confirming that evaluation and gating must operate strictly per-type.

That test compares raw score errors between quantities on different scales and is therefore weak evidence in either direction, but we report it rather than omit it. Establishing a transfer advantage over structural heuristics requires the stronger \texttt{Topo-QoS} baseline of Table~\ref{tab:7}, which this table does not yet include.

\noindent\textbf{The Neural Model is the Cheapest Stage, but Not the Whole Cost.} For a 2,000-component topology, HGT forward inference requires only $43.7\,\text{ms}$, while classical structural analysis takes $23.8\,\text{s}$ ($545\times$ slower). This ratio is a statement about where the cost sits inside the pipeline, not about the cost of evaluating an architecture: indices 0--17 of every node feature vector (Section~\ref{sec:3.4}) are betweenness, closeness, reverse PageRank, articulation and bridge scores produced by that same analysis stage, so the forward pass cannot run without it. End-to-end pre-deployment evaluation of a previously unseen 2,000-component architecture therefore costs approximately $24\,\text{s}$, of which the neural model is $0.2\%$. The $43.7\,\text{ms}$ figure is the marginal cost of re-scoring a graph that has already been analysed. The computational bottleneck resides entirely in deterministic graph algorithms ($O(|V|^2 + |V||E|)$). Computational overhead scales primarily with edge density: the 520-component Enterprise mesh requires $27.2\,\text{s}$ due to 3,245 structural edges, whereas a sparser 999-component system takes only $4.7\,\text{s}$. Across all corpus scenarios, the complete quality gate (structural analysis plus 18 anti-pattern detectors) runs in $0.02\,\text{s}$ to $27.4\,\text{s}$, well within standard pull-request CI/CD budgets.

\noindent\textbf{Computational Efficiency.} The quantity we measured is wall-clock time: the complete gate runs in $0.02$--$27.4\,\text{s}$ per architecture, against multi-seed discrete-event simulation sweeps that are substantially more expensive on the same corpus. We deliberately do not convert this into an energy or carbon figure. No physical power counters were instrumented in this study (Section~\ref{sec:8.2}), the mapping from CPU-seconds to joules depends on hardware and utilisation we did not control, and a latency ratio is not an energy ratio. What the measurements support is the narrower claim that pre-deployment architectural gating fits inside a pull-request CI/CD budget without a simulation cluster.
