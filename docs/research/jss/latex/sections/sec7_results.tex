\noindent\textbf{Key Insights for RQ1:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The learned models beat the learned baselines decisively; the untrained QoS baseline is the real competitor.} HGL-QoS is the best configuration overall ($\rho = 0.608$, $F_1@K = 0.414$), and its margin over both homogeneous GNNs is large and significant ($+0.246$ over GL-QoS, 8/8 folds, $p = 0.0078$). Against \emph{Topo-QoS}, however --- a training-free QoS-weighted centrality baseline --- the margin is $+0.037$, won in only 5 of 8 folds, and is \textbf{not statistically significant} ($p = 0.64$). We state this plainly: on out-of-distribution Application ranking, we cannot claim that heterogeneous graph learning outperforms a well-constructed structural baseline, only that it matches it while additionally supplying the typed attention and multi-task outputs the baseline cannot.
    \item \textbf{Critical-set detection is where the learned model separates.} HGL-QoS improves $F_1@K$ over Topo-QoS by $+0.034$ ($0.414$ vs.\ $0.380$, a $8.9\%$ relative gain) and over GL by $+0.177$. The advantage is real but far smaller than a pooled-population reading of the same experiment suggests.
    \item \textbf{QoS encoding is what carries the typed model out of distribution.} HGL-QoS leads HGL by $\Delta\rho = +0.169$, winning 7 of 8 folds ($p = 0.0156$) --- a larger and more consistent effect than the in-distribution comparison in Table~\ref{tab:6} suggests in the opposite direction. See Section~\ref{sec:7.3}.
    \item \textbf{The RM baseline is weakly predictive, not anti-predictive.} $\rho = 0.195$ under distribution shift: better than the untyped GL model and worse than every QoS-aware variant. RM's value lies in interpretable attribution rather than standalone ranking, but it is not noise.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{RQ2: Value of Typed Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:7.2}

To evaluate the contribution of node and edge typing, we compare relation-specific HGL against homogeneous GL across different validation regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In-Distribution:} Heterogeneous HGL leads homogeneous GL by $\Delta\rho = +0.116$ (0.725 vs.\ 0.609; not statistically significant, $p = 0.297$, 5/7 scenarios won). On familiar topologies, homogeneous GNNs can partially approximate type distinctions through structural degree signatures, so the typed model holds only a point-estimate edge. The contrast with the out-of-distribution result below is the finding: typing buys little when the topology is already known and a great deal when it is not.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Distribution (LOSO):} This is where typing decides the outcome. Heterogeneous HGL outperforms homogeneous GL by \textbf{$+0.353$} ($\rho = 0.439$ vs.\ $0.086$), winning \emph{all eight} folds (paired Wilcoxon, $p = 0.0078$), and the gap between the QoS-aware variants is $+0.246$ (HGL-QoS $0.608$ vs.\ GL-QoS $0.363$), also 8/8 and $p = 0.0078$. The untyped model very nearly fails to transfer at all on Application ranking: at $\rho = 0.086$ it is below even the unweighted structural baseline ($0.301$). When encountering unseen topologies, relation-specific message passing is not an incremental refinement but the difference between a model that generalizes and one that does not.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Empirical Edge-Removal Analysis}

We further probed edge criticality by simulating the removal of candidate relationship channels while keeping both endpoint components alive. On the \texttt{av\_system} topology across 50 candidate bridge edges, 46 edges exhibited zero downstream cascade impact, while the 4 edges with non-zero impact were direct library communication channels (\texttt{PUBLISHES\_TO} / \texttt{SUBSCRIBES\_TO}). This demonstrates that individual communication links are largely redundant, whereas component-level failures and shared library dependencies induce disproportionate systemic disruption.

\subsection{RQ3: Ablations and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:7.3}

\noindent\textbf{A note on what $\rho$ measures in this subsection.} Several sweeps below --- the AHP shrinkage $\lambda$, the topic-weight triple, the QoS sub-weights, the Morris and Dirichlet joint sweeps --- report RM's rank correlation against $I^*(v)$ as their outcome variable. RM is not proposed as a ranking model (Section~\ref{sec:4.1}), so within this subsection $\rho$ is used strictly as a \emph{sensitivity probe}: does perturbing a declared constant move a measurable quantity, and by how much relative to the gaps between predictor families elsewhere in this paper. A sweep finding that one setting yields higher $\rho$ than another is evidence about that constant's leverage on the score, not an optimality claim about RM as a ranker.

\subsubsection{QoS Feature Encoding}
Explicitly encoding continuous QoS attributes in the GNN (HGL-QoS) does not yield a uniform effect --- it trades a little in-distribution accuracy for a large out-of-distribution gain. The two directions are summarised below; note that they are measured under different protocols on different fold counts, though both are scored on the Application population.

\noindent The apparent contradiction between the two tables is a genuine regime effect, not an inconsistency. In-distribution, HGL-QoS \emph{trails} the base typed model, but the deficit is not statistically significant and is small relative to its own scatter (across-scenario $\sigma = 0.35$ for HGL-QoS against a $0.072$ gap); it is concentrated in two structurally atypical topologies, Hub-and-Spoke ($-0.238$) and AV ($-0.140$), with the remaining five within $\pm 0.084$. This is consistent with QoS features adding redundant signal, and mild overfitting risk, on topologies the model has already seen --- the derived \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} graph already embeds much of the same routing and coupling information through its edge weights.

Under LOSO the relationship reverses decisively and \emph{is} significant: HGL-QoS gains $+0.169$ and wins 7 of 8 folds. When the topology itself is unseen, structural degree signatures no longer transfer, whereas a declared QoS contract means the same thing in an unfamiliar architecture as in a familiar one --- \texttt{RELIABLE} and \texttt{PERSISTENT} carry their semantics across deployments in a way that a betweenness percentile does not. We therefore read QoS encoding as \emph{situational} rather than redundant: insurance against distribution shift, bought at a small and statistically insignificant in-distribution cost. For a practitioner the rule is simple --- use HGL when the target system resembles the training corpus, HGL-QoS when it does not, and HGL-QoS by default when that is unknown.

\subsubsection{Joint Sensitivity Across All Ten Weight Constants}
\label{sec:7.3.global}

The two sweeps above, like every other ablation in this section, vary one declared constant (or one convex combination on its own simplex) at a time. That is the standard one-at-a-time (OAT) design, and it cannot detect interactions between factors or say whether a factor found flat in isolation would stay flat if a second factor moved with it \cite{saltelli2008global}. We closed that gap directly, sweeping all ten hand-set weight constants jointly --- $(\beta, \alpha, \psi)$, $(w_{\text{rel}}, w_{\text{dur}}, w_{\text{prio}})$, the vertex power-mean exponent $p$, the library fan-out coefficient $\gamma$, the AHP shrinkage $\lambda$, and $r_\alpha$ --- using two complementary designs on six scenarios (\texttt{enterprise\_system} excluded on cost grounds; its structural analysis costs roughly $15\times$ any other scenario per evaluation point).

\textbf{Morris elementary-effects screening} \cite{morris1991factorial,campolongo2007effective} takes 10 random trajectories through the full ten-dimensional hyper-rectangle each factor's plausible range spans (110 evaluations), deliberately \emph{not} constraining the two convex-combination groups to a unit sum --- this is the tool for finding interactions and non-linearities an on-simplex sweep cannot see. It ranks factors by $\mu^*$, the mean absolute elementary effect on mean $\rho$ against $I^*(v)$:

\noindent The AHP shrinkage $\lambda$ and the Reliability-Availability blend $r_\alpha$ dominate, consistent with the two OAT sweeps above (Section~\ref{sec:7.3}, this section) already showing $\lambda$ as the more consequential of the two intra-dimension parameters. The topic-weight triple and the QoS sub-weight triple now sit in a comparable, modest band ($\mu^* \in [0.012, 0.019]$) rather than one dominating another by an order of magnitude, confirming at the joint level what the corrected term-contribution figures above show at the formula level: none of these six factors is individually load-bearing, and none is now negligible by construction either. $p$ and $\gamma$ are the least influential factors in the whole design, both roughly an order of magnitude below the topic-weight/QoS-sub-weight band; $\sigma$ relative to $\mu^*$ is largest for $\lambda$ and $\alpha$, indicating their effect on $\rho$ depends more on where the other nine factors sit --- the specific kind of interaction an OAT sweep cannot diagnose, though its magnitude here remains within the same order as $\mu^*$ itself rather than dominating it.

\textbf{Dirichlet simplex sampling} answers the sharper, practically relevant question: over the realistic space of "declared budgets" --- points that respect the unit-sum constraint on both convex-combination groups simultaneously, holding the other six factors at shipped values --- how much does ranking accuracy and the ranked shortlist itself actually vary. Over 100 draws, mean $\rho = 0.243$ (shipped $\rho = 0.252$), $\text{sd} = 0.006$, range $[0.229, 0.260]$, a 90\% interval of $[0.231, 0.253]$. Rank-stability against the shipped ranking is high across the same draws: mean Kendall $\tau = 0.899$ (worst draw $0.791$) and mean top-20\%-of-population Jaccard overlap $= 0.825$ (worst draw $0.699$). The declared topic-weight and QoS-sub-weight budgets are, jointly and on the realistic simplex, not a lever on either the score or the shortlist it produces.

\subsubsection{Convergent Validity Across Simulation Oracles}

To test construct validity, we compared node criticality rankings across three of the four component-level oracles from Section~\ref{sec:5.1} --- the topological cascade reachability oracle $I^*(v)$ (\texttt{FaultInjector}), the multi-metric composite oracle $I_{\text{comp}}(v)$ (\texttt{FailureSimulator}), and the discrete-event queue-flow simulator $I_{\text{dyn}}(v)$ (\texttt{MessageFlowSimulator}) --- over seven synthetic scenarios and five seeds, on the Application population. The fourth, the Maintainability-only $I_M(v)$, is excluded here because it targets a different dimension rather than a competing reliability ranking; its own construct-validity limitation is discussed separately (Section~\ref{sec:8.2}).

\noindent\textbf{Ordering converges; the critical set does not.} The behavioural queue simulator and the topological cascade oracle agree strongly on ordering ($\rho = 0.883$, never below $0.756$ on any scenario). Because the two are constructed on different principles --- one observing delivered message rates under load, the other reachability over edges --- this agreement is not reducible to a shared construction artifact, and it is the strongest convergent evidence available to us that $I^*(v)$ tracks dynamic service disruption rather than only graph topology. The composite oracle is the outlier, agreeing only moderately with either ($\rho \approx 0.47$).

Set-level agreement is a different and weaker story, and we report it as the binding limitation rather than burying it. Top-$K$ Jaccard is $0.42$ for the strongest pair and $0.31$ for the others, against $0.111$ expected under independent rankings. We ran two controls before interpreting this. It is \emph{not} a tie-breaking artifact: admitting every component tied with the $K$-th moves the figure by at most $0.005$. And it is not simply label noise, though noise is a substantial part of it --- the labeler's own seed-to-seed self-agreement spans top-$K$ Jaccard $0.44$--$1.00$, so one oracle compared against \emph{itself} reaches only $0.44$ in its worst scenario. Top-$K$ set identity at $K = 0.2n$ is therefore an intrinsically unstable statistic, and the cross-oracle values sit just below the floor a single oracle achieves against its own reruns. The defensible claim is about ranking, not about membership of a fixed-size critical set.

\noindent\textbf{A falsified hypothesis, reported.} We tested whether weighting both topological oracles by the same $w(t)$ makes them converge --- the mechanism by which QoS weighting would be a construct-validity improvement rather than merely a modelling choice. It does not: $\rho(I_{\text{comp}}, I^*)$ is $0.468$ with QoS weighting and $0.477$ without it ($\Delta\rho = -0.009$; Jaccard $0.307$ against $0.306$). Shared weighting has no measurable effect on inter-oracle agreement in either direction, and we record the negative result rather than leaving the artifact uncited.

\noindent\textbf{The two arms of that test are not comparably strong.} Disabling QoS weighting
removes $I_{\text{comp}}$'s severity term entirely (every topic reverts to a flat weight of $1$),
but on $I^*$ it only removes a ladder multiplier that a $[0,1]$ clamp already suppresses whenever a
component's feed loss is at or near saturation --- precisely the high-impact regime a ranking
metric is most sensitive to. Toggling that ladder shifts $I^*$'s own labels by a mean absolute
$0.064$ across our ten scenarios (range $0.012$--$0.145$; a shared $w(t)$-proportional treatment
shifts them by only $0.013$), so the null result above bounds the effect of \emph{this} treatment
rather than of QoS-aware labelling in general. The one untested arm where a QoS weight is not
structurally clipped is $I_{\text{dyn}}$'s delivery-rate label, which we leave to future work.

\subsubsection{Domain-Specific Weighting Sensitivity}

We investigated the impact of Context of Use reweighting $\vec{\omega} = [q_R, q_M]^\top$ across 10 evaluation scenarios by sweeping the composite reliability weight $w_R$ over its full range and reading off three points on that one curve: the shipped static default ($w_R = 0.80$), an unweighted equal split ($w_R = 0.50$), and the domain-derived value per scenario. The three are statistically indistinguishable: mean $\rho = 0.353$ (static), $0.349$ (equal), $0.347$ (domain-derived), so domain derivation trails equal weighting by $\Delta\rho = -0.001$ and the static default by $\Delta\rho = -0.006$. Neither difference is meaningful, and the sweep explains why: over the \emph{entire} $w_R \in [0, 1]$ range mean $\rho$ moves only from $0.341$ to $0.365$, a total spread of $0.024$. The composite weight is simply not a lever on ranking. Two structural facts compound this: across the declared domains the derived $w_R$ lies in $[0.70, 0.76]$, i.e.\ at most $0.10$ from the static default, and mean Kendall rank fidelity between domain-derived and static rankings is $\tau = 0.974$ --- the two weightings order components almost identically. We therefore read this ablation as confirming the scoping in Section~\ref{sec:4.1}: operational Context of Use reweighting is an \emph{attributional} mechanism --- explaining why a component matters in stakeholder terms --- and not a ranking-improvement device. It should not be reported as either an accuracy gain or an accuracy loss.

\subsubsection{Threshold and Normalization Sensitivity}

We swept 7 scenarios $\times$ a cascade propagation-threshold parameter $\in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.35, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$ controlling the ground-truth oracle's eligibility cutoff for cascade propagation, and separately swept 3 label-normalization techniques (robust, min-max, standard) holding the threshold fixed. Ranking performance is more sensitive to the propagation threshold ($\Delta\rho = 0.102$ across the extreme settings; mean $\rho$ rises from $0.177$ at threshold $0$ to $0.278$ at threshold $0.5$ and plateaus thereafter, the shipped $0.35$ sitting inside that plateau at $0.274$) than to normalization choice ($\Delta\rho = 0.022$; robust $0.267$ against $0.244$ for both min-max and standard). The threshold is thus the more consequential free parameter of the two, and the shipped setting is on the flat part of its curve rather than at a tuned peak. A small spread under either sweep means the reported ordering is stable under that parameter --- it is not, by itself, evidence that the ordering is \emph{correct}.

\subsubsection{Availability Was Measuring Almost Nothing, and Why}
\label{sec:7.3.availability}

The Availability sub-characteristic $A(v)$ (Section~\ref{sec:4}) was, in the version of the pipeline these results were first computed on, driven almost entirely by a single component of a five-term formula that fires vanishingly rarely on this corpus. $AP_c$(directed), Bridge Ratio, and $CDI$ are all articulation-point-gated: a node that is not a Tarjan cut vertex receives exactly $0.0$ on all three, by construction. On six of the eight LOSO scenarios --- including the 350-component Enterprise topology --- \emph{zero} Application-layer components are articulation points, so all three terms vanish identically for the entire population and $A(v)$ collapses to its remaining $0.05 \cdot w(v)$ QoS-weight term: $A_{\max}$ sits at exactly $0.0500$ (Enterprise, Av, Microservices, Hub-and-Spoke) or within rounding of it (ATM $0.0492$, Financial $0.0455$), with standard deviation $\sigma_A \in [0.0145, 0.0398]$ against $\sigma_{FT} \in [0.147, 0.171]$ for the co-dimension it is supposed to complement. The rule-based SPOF anti-pattern detector, which reads the same underlying articulation-point membership, corroborates this directly: mean $F_1 = 0.0042$ across the eight scenarios, with zero predicted SPOFs in seven of eight.

This is not evidence that single points of failure are absent from these architectures --- only that the hard cut-vertex test cannot see them in a topology this redundant. The corpus's own impact-defined labels contradict the null: on the IoT scenario, the SPOF detector achieves precision $1.0$ at recall $0.017$ against the simulated-impact ground truth, meaning the one component it does flag is a genuine failure-critical node, and it is simply blind to the rest. A component that is redundantly but load-bearingly connected --- e.g.\ the sole active publisher into a topic with several structurally-possible-but-unused alternate routes --- fragments nothing when removed, so it is invisible to a binary cut-vertex test, even though removing it measurably degrades reachability for the rest of the system.

We therefore replaced $CDI$'s cut-vertex gate with a continuous redundancy-deficit measure: for every node in the graph's main connected component (not only articulation points), $CDI(v)$ is the fractional increase in average shortest-path length from a fixed sample of high-degree sources when $v$ is removed, capped at $1.0$ on genuine fragmentation. A true cut vertex remains at or near the ceiling --- the prior semantics are the limiting case of the new ones, not replaced by them --- while a redundant-but-load-bearing node now registers a graded, nonzero score instead of an exact $0$. We rebalanced $A(v)$'s AHP-derived weights accordingly, promoting $CDI$ from a minor term ($a_{CDI} = 0.10$, geometric-mean rank 4 of 5) to parity with the hard cut-vertex term ($a_{CDI} = a_{AP_c} = 0.2563$, Saaty judgment: equal importance), on the grounds that both now measure genuine removal-impact severity at different levels of graduation, and shrank the QoS-weighted interaction term ($a_{QSPOF}$: $0.25 \to 0.1998$) to bound its known overlap with the standalone $AP_c$ term. Recomputing $A(v)$ under the new formula moves $\sigma_A$ to $[0.0254, 0.0530]$ across the same eight scenarios --- roughly $1.6$--$2.0\times$ its prior range --- and $A_{\max}$ off the exact $0.05 \cdot w(v)$ ceiling in every scenario (e.g.\ Enterprise: $0.0500 \to 0.0878$). $A(v)$ is no longer a rescaled QoS weight with zero structural content, though it remains, correctly, a narrower-spread signal than $FT(v)$: genuine fragility is meant to be the exception in a well-architected system, not the common case.

\subsubsection{Anti-Pattern Detection and CI/CD Quality Gates}

Validating our rule-based architectural anti-pattern catalog against the multi-metric composite oracle $I_{\text{comp}}(v)$ yielded a mean detection $F_1 = 0.3781$ across 8 system scenarios --- but $F_1$ alone understates what the catalog does: mean precision is $0.239$ against mean recall $0.900$, meaning the catalog flags $94.2\%$ of all components on average and trades precision for near-exhaustive coverage of true positives. This is a deliberate high-recall CI/CD posture (a missed critical component is more costly than a false positive requiring manual triage) rather than an accuracy shortfall, but should be read as such rather than via $F_1$ in isolation. One detector, \texttt{DEEP\_PIPELINE}, is excluded from this evaluation: it enumerates every simple source-to-sink path and does not terminate within ten minutes on the 50-application Healthcare topology --- the blowup is itself a reportable scalability limit of exhaustive path enumeration, not a measurement gap. Analysis execution times for the remaining 18 detectors ranged from $0.04\,\text{s}$ (small scenarios, 50 nodes) to $54.85\,\text{s}$ (enterprise event meshes, 300 nodes), confirming that SaG runs comfortably within standard pre-commit and pull-request CI/CD quality gate budgets.

We additionally stratify the RM composite's rank correlation by node type: $\rho = 0.503$ (Application), $0.395$ (Broker), $0.142$ (Node), while the \emph{pooled} correlation across all types is $\rho = 0.028$ --- outside the per-type range entirely. This is a Simpson's-paradox effect (aggregating across populations with different scales/base rates reverses the within-group trend), and it means the pooled figure is not a summary of the per-type figures: only the stratified, per-type numbers should be quoted when discussing RM's structural predictive power.

\subsubsection{Using RM Correctly: Score Within a Node Type}
\label{sec:7.3.usingrm}

The stratification above is not only a reporting convention for this paper; it is a usage rule for the framework, and getting it wrong changes which components an architect is told to fix. We therefore state it operationally.

\noindent\textbf{The rule.} Score, tier and rank components \emph{within} a node type. Never derive a single ranking, or a single criticality threshold, from a population that mixes Applications, Brokers, Topics, Infrastructure Nodes and Libraries. Their criticality scores differ in scale and in base rate, so a shared threshold measures type membership as much as it measures risk.

\noindent\textbf{The mechanism is already in the framework.} The layer projections of Section~\ref{sec:3.3} are the stratification device: $\pi_{\text{infra}}$ reports Nodes only, $\pi_{\text{mw}}$ reports Brokers only, and $\pi_{\text{app}}$ reports Applications and Libraries. Only $\pi_{\text{system}}$ spans all five types. Practitioners should read $\pi_{\text{system}}$ as a structural and visualisation view of the whole architecture, and take ranking or gating decisions from the single-type layers.

\noindent\textbf{What pooling costs, measured.} We classified every component in the eight-scenario corpus twice --- once against one box-plot over the whole system layer, once within its own node type --- and compared the resulting CRITICAL/HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW/MINIMAL tiers. Across 1{,}619 components, \textbf{62.8\% land in a different tier}, and \textbf{19.0\% cross the CRITICAL/HIGH boundary} that determines whether a component is surfaced for action at all. The effect is stable across scenarios (tier changes $51$--$69\%$, boundary crossings $14$--$22\%$), so it is a property of mixing the populations rather than of any one topology. The direction is systematic: pooling suppresses the top of whichever type carries the lower score scale, because the higher-scaled type occupies the upper quartile outright.

\noindent\textbf{What we changed.} The classifier now derives quartiles and fences within each node type, and the per-dimension criticality flags use the same fence that produced the component's tier, so flag and tier cannot disagree. Types with too few members for stable quartiles fall back to the pooled fence rather than being scored on a handful of samples. The residual case is $\pi_{\text{app}}$, which still pools Applications with Libraries; their LOSO correlations overlap ($0.195$ against $0.105$), so we retain the pairing and note it as a scope condition rather than a defect.

\noindent\textbf{The GNN is the cheapest stage.} Inference on a 2{,}000-component system takes $44\,\text{ms}$ --- a handful of sparse matrix products whose cost grows close to linearly in $|E|$ --- while the structural analysis preceding it takes $24\,\text{s}$. The ratio widens with scale rather than narrowing, from $12\times$ at 249 components to $545\times$ at 1{,}998. Any effort spent making this framework scale should therefore go to the classical graph metrics, not to the model: betweenness is $O(|V||E|)$ and the directed articulation score and closeness are $O(|V|(|V|+|E|))$, giving an overall $O(|V|^2 + |V||E|)$. One mitigation already ships, with the connectivity-degradation term switching to deterministic top-50 core sampling above $|V| = 300$.

\noindent\textbf{Cost tracks edges, not components.} The corpus scenarios make this concrete: the 520-component Enterprise mesh takes $27.2\,\text{s}$ to analyse while a \emph{denser-in-name-only} 999-component generated system takes $4.7\,\text{s}$, because the former carries 3{,}245 structural edges against a far sparser fan-out per component. Practitioners sizing a CI/CD budget should estimate from $|E|$, or from $|V| \cdot |E|$, rather than from component count. Within the paper's corpus the whole gate --- analysis plus all 18 evaluated detectors --- runs in $0.02\,\text{s}$ (29 components) to $27.4\,\text{s}$ (520 components), comfortably inside a pull-request budget.

\noindent\textbf{What we have not measured, and do not claim.} Nothing above 2{,}000 components has been timed, and nothing here should be extrapolated past it; all figures are single-threaded CPU, with no GPU measurement; and one detector, \texttt{DEEP\_PIPELINE}, is excluded throughout because exhaustive source-to-sink path enumeration does not terminate within ten minutes even at 50 applications (Section~\ref{sec:7.3}). That detector is the one component of the framework known \emph{not} to scale, and its exclusion is a limitation rather than a tuning choice. Training cost, for completeness: the full seven-variant, eight-fold, five-seed leave-one-scenario-out sweep reported in Table~\ref{tab:7} took approximately 45 minutes per learned variant on CPU.
