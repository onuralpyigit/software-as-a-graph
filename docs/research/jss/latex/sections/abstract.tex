Publish--subscribe middleware obscures the paths through which failures propagate, yet architects must identify critical components before deployment, when no telemetry exists. We present Software-as-a-Graph (SaG), which derives explicit dependency graphs from deployment manifests to evaluate when graph learning improves cascade-impact ranking. We compare graph neural networks, hybrids, and analytical and learned simulator approximations with training-free rankings on twelve synthetic architectures under leave-one-scenario-out evaluation against three failure simulators, and zero-shot on five hand-authored models of open-source systems. A reference criterion distinguishes rankings that restate a simulator's propagation rule from those that predict it.

While the primary registered contrast was null, graph attention on the derived dependency graph reaches Spearman's $\rho = 0.748$ against a reachability simulator, whereas pure learned models on the raw multigraph do not outperform a training-free baseline. The learned model is not statistically distinguishable from counting direct dependents ($\rho = 0.764$), though equivalence within $\pm 0.05$ is not established. For a queue-flow simulator requiring $12.7$ CPU-hours to label the corpus, a rate-weighted first-order approximation reaches $\rho = 0.830$ in milliseconds, exceeding a learned approximation trained on simulator labels ($\rho = 0.799$). Hybrids correcting a closed-form prior outperform that baseline, and learned models transfer zero-shot better than it ($\rho \approx 0.81$ vs.\ $0.53$), driven by separating inert components. No learned model exceeded analytical rankings aligned with the simulator.

All findings concern agreement with failure simulators, not prediction of physical failures. Representation mattered more than model complexity, highlighting the importance of evaluating learned methods against analytical baselines aligned with the simulator.
