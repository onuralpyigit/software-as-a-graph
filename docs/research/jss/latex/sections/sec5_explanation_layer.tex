\section{The Explanation Layer: Standards-Grounded Criticality Attribution}
\label{sec:5}

The predictor described in Section~\ref{sec:4} identifies the concentration of risk but does not address remediation strategies. A component may be deemed critical due to its status as an unreplicated single point of failure, its role as an error-propagating cascade hub, or as a high-coupling maintainability bottleneck. Each of these structural causes necessitates distinct remediation approaches, such as broker replication, circuit breaker insertion, or refactoring of module dependencies. This section formalizes the diagnostic layer responsible for attributing these causes. SaG decomposes component and relationship criticality into a standards-grounded quality profile, calculated using the same typed node properties (Section~\ref{sec:3.4}) but without parameter sharing with the neural predictor, and applies this profile to flagged components through triage rather than data flow (Figure~\ref{fig:1}).

The evidential status of this layer is explicitly stated: it represents an unvalidated design contribution intended for qualitative attribution rather than quantitative ranking. As demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:7.1}, its standalone rank correlation is low ($\rho = 0.205$, consistently below unweighted centrality), its elicited AHP weights underperform compared to a uniform prior (Section~\ref{sec:7.3}), and no human-subject studies have yet assessed developer adoption. This layer maps topological properties to standardized ISO/IEC concepts.

\subsection{Grounding in ISO/IEC Standards}
\label{sec:5.1}

In accordance with \textbf{ISO/IEC 25010:2023} \cite{iso25010_2023} and \textbf{ISO/IEC 25019:2023} \cite{iso25019_2023}, SaG formalizes two primary criticality dimensions: \textbf{Component Criticality ($D_1$)} (service loss upon component failure) and \textbf{Relationship Criticality ($D_2$)} (service decline upon channel severance).

Criticality is assessed across two orthogonal characteristics: \textbf{Reliability ($R$)} and \textbf{Maintainability ($M$)}. Reliability is further divided into \textbf{Fault Tolerance ($FT$)} (error-propagation reachability on $G^\top$, evaluated using Reverse PageRank, in-degree, and cascade depth potential to inform redundancy and circuit breaker strategies) and \textbf{Availability ($A$)} (exposure to single points of failure, evaluated using directed articulation points, bridge ratios, and connectivity degradation to inform replication strategies). Maintainability ($M$) assesses structural coupling and code-level complexity, utilizing metrics such as betweenness, QoS-weighted fan-out, code quality penalties, and clustering to guide decoupling and refactoring. Supplementary Table~S3 provides a summary of this partition, mapping each ISO/IEC sub-characteristic to its corresponding graph metrics and remediation roles. Safety and security considerations that require specialized hazard logs are excluded from purely structural topology analysis.

\subsection{Composite Quality Score Formulation}
\label{sec:5.2}

Rank-normalize all raw metrics to $[0, 1]$ within the graph. Quality sub-characteristics are formulated hierarchically using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) \cite{saaty1980ahp}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fault Tolerance ($FT(v)$):} Evaluates error cascade potential on transpose graph $G_{\text{analysis}}^\top$:

    $FT(v) = 0.45 \cdot \text{RPR}(v) + 0.30 \cdot \text{Deg}_{\text{in}}(v) + 0.25 \cdot \text{CDPot}_{\text{enh}}(v)$,

    where $\text{RPR}(v)$ is Reverse PageRank (RPR), $\text{Deg}_{\text{in}}(v) = d_{\text{in}}(v)/(|V|-1)$ is normalized in-degree, and $\text{CDPot}_{\text{enh}}(v) = \text{depth}(v) / \max_{u \in V} \text{depth}(u)$ is the normalized cascade depth potential, measuring the longest reachable directed failure-propagation chain from $v$ on $G_{\text{analysis}}^\top$.

    \item \textbf{Availability ($A(v)$):} Identifies structural single points of failure across five terms:

    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:availability}
    A(v) = 0.2563 \cdot \text{AP}_c^{\text{dir}}(v) + 0.1998 \cdot \text{QSPOF}(v) + 0.1998 \cdot \text{BR}(v) + 0.2563 \cdot \text{CDI}(v) + 0.0878 \cdot w(v)
    \end{equation}

    where $\text{AP}_c^{\text{dir}}(v)$ is Directed Articulation Point (AP) severity, $\text{QSPOF}(v)$ is QoS-weighted Single Point of Failure (QSPOF) severity, $\text{BR}(v)$ is Bridge Ratio (BR), $\text{CDI}(v)$ is Connectivity Degradation Index (CDI), and $w(v)$ is intrinsic QoS weight.

    \item \textbf{Reliability ($R(v)$):} Blends Fault Tolerance and Availability: $R(v) = r_{\text{FT}} \cdot FT(v) + (1 - r_{\text{FT}}) \cdot A(v)$, with $r_{\text{FT}} = 0.36$. The intra-dimension weights apply $\lambda = 0.70$ shrinkage blending with a uniform prior. Because comparison matrices are rank-one by construction (Supplementary Section~S4), these weights represent documented conventions rather than independently elicited consensus.

    \item \textbf{Maintainability ($M(v)$):} Blends structural coupling with static code analysis:

    $M(v) = 0.35 \cdot \text{BT}(v) + 0.30 \cdot w_{\text{out}}(v) + 0.15 \cdot \text{CQP}(v) + 0.12 \cdot \text{CouplingRisk}_{\text{enh}}(v) + 0.08 \cdot (1 - \text{CC}(v))$,

    where $\text{BT}(v)$ is Betweenness Centrality (BT), $w_{\text{out}}(v)$ is QoS-weighted efferent coupling, $\text{CQP}(v)$ is Code Quality Penalty (CQP), and $\text{CC}(v)$ is local Clustering Coefficient (CC).
\end{enumerate}

The baseline composite quality score integrates both dimensions: $Q(v) = 0.80 \cdot R(v) + 0.20 \cdot M(v)$. When evaluated under an ISO/IEC 25019 Context of Use vector $\vec{\omega} = [q_R, q_M]^\top$, the score is dynamically reweighted: $Q_{\text{domain}}(v) = q_R \cdot R(v) + q_M \cdot M_{\text{static}}(v)$. Components are partitioned into Tukey tiers: CRITICAL ($Q > Q_3 + 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR}$), HIGH, MEDIUM, and MINIMAL. A high $A$ combined with low $FT$ indicates a single point of failure that necessitates replication, while high $FT$ identifies an error-cascade hub that requires circuit breakers (Section~\ref{sec:8.4}).

\subsection{Prescriptive Remediation and Counterfactual Verification}
\label{sec:5.3}

After root causes are attributed, automated refactoring operators generate candidate repair manifests, such as broker replication, circuit breaker insertion, or topic decoupling. An \texttt{EditVerifier} constructs the mutated graph $G'$ in memory and counterfactually re-simulates multi-threshold cascades. Candidate repairs are accepted only if they reduce systemic impact beyond simulation seed noise ($\Delta \bar{I}_{\text{comp}} > \kappa \cdot \sigma_{\text{seed}}$, $\kappa \ge 1.0$) and do not introduce new articulation points. This counterfactual verification loop is presented to illustrate the architectural pattern linking diagnosis to remediation. The present study does not evaluate standalone empirical claims regarding prescriptive repair efficacy or production patch synthesis, and automated refactoring benchmarks are reserved for future work.
