\hypertarget{interpretable-attribution-as-a-baseline}{%
\section{Interpretable Attribution as a Baseline}\label{interpretable-attribution-as-a-baseline}}

\label{sec:4}

Centrality answers \emph{whether} a component is important with a single number; an architect
choosing between a replica, a reroute, and a decoupling refactor needs to know \emph{why}. This
section presents the framework's interpretable diagnostic: a decomposition of each component's
criticality into two top-level quality dimensions --- Reliability and Maintainability (RM) --- each
computed from disjoint structural metrics and combined into a composite score. Reliability is itself
hierarchical, blending two sub-characteristics, Fault Tolerance and Availability:
\(R(v) = \alpha \cdot FT(v) + (1-\alpha)\cdot A(v)\), \(\alpha = 0.36\). Grounded in \textbf{ISO/IEC
25019:2023 (Quality-in-Use)} \cite{iso25019_2023}, criticality represents the counterfactual loss of
beneficialness, freedom from risk, and acceptability that stakeholders would experience if an
architectural element failed.

\hypertarget{two-dimensions-and-formal-definitions}{%
\subsection{Two Dimensions and Formal Definitions}\label{two-dimensions-and-formal-definitions}}

\label{sec:4.1}

Two definitions fix what is being scored. \textbf{D1 --- component criticality}: the degree to which
the failure, latency, or degradation of a component, directly or transitively, reduces the system's
capacity to let its stakeholders achieve their goals with beneficialness, freedom from risk, and
acceptability, within its operational context. \textbf{D2 --- relationship criticality}: the same
degree of loss for the disruption of one specific dependency, with \emph{both endpoint components
remaining operational} --- the clause that isolates the partial-outage case from the total-outage one.
Both are realised per analytical layer as a map onto the RM vector and its composite.

For components, the two dimensions are \textbf{orthogonal by construction}: each raw structural metric
feeds exactly one of \(FT\), \(A\), or \(M\), never more --- a deliberate design constraint, not an
empirical observation, since allowing a metric into two terms would silently inflate its weight
relative to the stated weighting (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). Metric-level orthogonality is what makes the
breakdown legible: a pure single point of failure scores high on the \(A\) sub-term but low on \(FT\)
and \(M\); a god-component scores high on \(M\); a cascade hub scores high on the \(FT\) sub-term. The
\emph{shape} of the profile --- which sub-term of \(R\) dominates, and how \(R\) compares to \(M\) ---
names the failure mode.

Two further formal definitions do real work in what follows. \textbf{Criticality is a
consequence, not a risk (D3)}: no RM dimension estimates how \emph{probable} a component's failure is,
only how much is lost \emph{given} that it does --- ranking \(u\) above \(v\) says that losing \(u\)
hurts more, not that \(u\) is more likely to be lost, and restricting the construct to consequence is
precisely what makes it computable pre-deployment, since consequence follows from architecture while
likelihood follows from behaviour that does not yet exist. \textbf{Criticality is relative, not
absolute (D4)}: every score and tier is relative to the score distribution of the system being
analysed (tiers are box-plot thresholds, Section~\ref{sec:4.2}) and to the analytical layer, so
criticality values are \textbf{not comparable across systems or layers} --- a well-designed redundant
system still has a CRITICAL tier, and a system full of SPOFs still has a MINIMAL one. D4 constrains
how this paper may aggregate: any figure computed over more than one scenario must be formed from
within-scenario ranks or per-scenario statistics, never from raw scores pooled across systems
(Section~\ref{sec:5.4}, Section~\ref{sec:8.5} carry the corresponding scoping statements).

The framework also gives inter-component dependencies the same two-dimensional attribution as
components, so that a partial outage --- one link down, both endpoints healthy --- is scored rather
than inferred from endpoint scores. We do not develop this relationship-level construction further
here: by the framework's own independence guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:5.3}), it cannot currently be
validated against the edge-removal measurement of Section~\ref{sec:8.2}, which operates on a
structurally disjoint population (Section~\ref{sec:9.3}).

\hypertarget{the-composite-score-classification-and-determinism}{%
\subsection{The Composite Score, Classification, and Determinism}\label{the-composite-score-classification-and-determinism}}

\label{sec:4.2}

All metric inputs are rank-normalized to \([0,1]\). \textbf{Fault Tolerance} (\(FT\)), Reliability's
first sub-term, is driven by Reverse PageRank on the transpose \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} graph \(G^\top\)
(the failure-propagation direction, since edges point dependent → dependency), blended with in-degree
and a cascade-depth potential amplified by multi-path coupling, with a fan-out form dispatched for
Topic nodes, which have no \texttt{DEPENDS\_ON} in-degree. \textbf{Availability} (\(A\)), the second
sub-term, is driven by the directed articulation score (rather than the undirected version, which both
over- and under-reports in pub-sub graphs), amplified by QoS weight so a SPOF carrying critical
traffic scores as doubly severe. These blend into \textbf{Reliability}:

\begin{equation}
R(v) = \alpha \cdot FT(v) + (1-\alpha)\cdot A(v), \quad \alpha = 0.36
\end{equation}

\textbf{Maintainability} is driven by betweenness centrality, QoS-weighted out-degree, and a Code
Quality Penalty built from the ingested SonarQube metrics of Section~\ref{sec:3.1} --- numerically
unchanged from its role in the retired four-dimension composite. \(R\) and \(M\) combine into

\begin{equation}
Q(v) = w_R\,R(v) + w_M\,M(v)
\end{equation}

under declared weights \((w_R, w_M) = (0.80, 0.20)\) --- algebraically derived from the retired
four-dimension AHP composite \((A, R, M, V) = (0.43, 0.24, 0.17, 0.16)\) by folding \(A\)'s weight into
\(R\)'s (\(\alpha = 0.24/(0.24+0.43) \approx 0.36\)) and renormalising the remaining two
(\(w_R = (0.24+0.43)/0.84 \approx 0.80\), \(w_M = 0.17/0.84 \approx 0.20\)) --- a design judgement
written on Saaty's 1--9 scale and audited for Analytic Hierarchy Process consistency
\cite{saaty1980ahp} (\(\mathrm{CR} \le 0.10\)) at the sub-characteristic level rather than freshly
elicited --- adapted per system toward its aggregate QoS profile, the computable form of D1's ``within
its operational context'' clause. The audit is confined to the sub-characteristic level for a
structural reason, not by oversight: a consistency ratio is defined as zero for \(n \le 2\), so a
two-criterion comparison between Reliability and Maintainability would be consistent by construction
and could not be audited at all. We therefore hold no composite comparison matrix, and the composite
split is reported as declared rather than as an audited elicitation.

We classify with an adaptive box-plot rule applied independently to each dimension and to the
composite (CRITICAL: \(Q > Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}\), down to MINIMAL: \(Q \le Q_1\), with a
percentile fallback below \(n=12\)), so that a component can be CRITICAL on Reliability yet MINIMAL
on Maintainability --- telling the architect to add redundancy rather than refactor for modularity.
Attribution is fully deterministic: every input to \(Q(v)\) is a structural metric of
\(G_{\text{analysis}}\), none derives from the discrete-event simulation that produces the
ground-truth labels used to evaluate the framework (Section~\ref{sec:5.1}) --- the
\textbf{independence guarantee} that makes a measured correlation between \(Q(v)\) and simulated
impact evidence of genuine predictive content rather than leakage.

\textbf{We report the sensitivity of this weighting, and it mildly favours the calibrated judgement.}
Sweeping a shrinkage parameter \(\lambda\) that blends the AHP-derived \emph{intra}-dimension term
weights --- the \(FT\), \(A\), and \(M\) internal term weights, not the composite \((w_R, w_M)\), which
is a declared constant and \(\lambda\)-invariant by construction --- toward a uniform prior shows no
plateau at any value: mean \(\rho\) against simulated impact rises monotonically as \(\lambda\) moves
from 0 (uniform intra-dimension weights) to 1 (the raw AHP judgement), with the raw judgement
outperforming uniform weighting by \(\approx 0.032\,\rho\) and the shipped default (\(\lambda=0.70\))
sitting partway along that curve (Section~\ref{sec:8.3}, Table~\ref{tab:11}). The effect is small
(spread \(\approx 0.045\,\rho\) across the full range, all values near-zero-to-slightly-negative) but
consistent in direction, unlike an earlier version of this sweep under the retired four-dimension
composite, which found the opposite sign. We draw a corresponding conclusion about the contribution:
\textbf{the value of the RM decomposition remains primarily attribution, not ranking accuracy} --- the
sensitivity result no longer argues against the calibrated weighting, but the effect size is too small
to justify leaning on it for ranking alone. A two-dimensional profile explains \emph{why} a component
ranks where it does and routes the finding to the engineering role equipped to act on it --- a
Reliability issue and a Maintainability issue call for different remediations even at identical
composite scores, and within Reliability itself the \(FT\) and \(A\) sub-terms further distinguish a
cascade hub from a structural single point of failure --- and that explanatory function is unaffected
by the weighting result. A practitioner optimising for ranking alone gains a small amount by leaning
toward the raw AHP judgement rather than uniform intra-dimension weights, though the effect is too
small to be decisive; one who needs to know \emph{why} a component is critical needs the profile,
whatever the weights.
