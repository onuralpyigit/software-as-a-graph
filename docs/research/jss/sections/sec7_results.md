## 6.1 RQ1: Ranking Accuracy of Learned Engines

**Summary.** *Derived dependency graphs enable highly accurate cascade-impact ranking: the graph attention network (`GAT-P-QoS`) reaches $\rho = 0.748$ against the primary reachability oracle $I^*$. Causal ablations reveal that it achieves this strong performance by distilling the underlying degree distribution (losing $0.14$ without degree features; §6.2), matching the first-order reference level ($0.764$, indistinguishable; not equivalent within $\pm 0.05$). On the expensive queue-flow oracle $I_{\text{dyn}}$ ($12.7$ CPU-hours), a learned surrogate over dependency-graph features achieves $\rho = 0.799$, significantly outperforming every closed-form approximation ($0.706$, Holm $p = 0.019$) and capturing declared QoS signals ($+0.095$). Under multi-criteria failure conditions ($I_{\text{comp}}$), hybrid engines maintain robust stability ($0.58$) where pure neural models collapse ($0.144$–$0.334$). On the raw multigraph, the registered primary contrast (`HGT-QoS` vs. `Topo-QoS`, $0.553$; corrected $0.533$) was null ($\Delta\rho = +0.069$, $p = 0.266$). Recovering $80\%$ of the true top-$20\%$ set requires flagging the top $40\%$ by `GAT-P-QoS`.*

Learned engines are trained on eleven scenarios and scored on the twelfth; training-free rankers are simply scored on each of the twelve (26 to 300 Applications, $K$ between 5 and 60). Table 5 reports all of them against $I^*$, with the reference rankings of §5.2 in a separate block: they restate $I^*$’s rule, so their rows measure the circularity and carry no contrast.

**The reference level.** By Remark 1, `InDeg` is publish–subscribe afferent coupling [26, 27, 46] and counts exactly the Applications that $I^*$’s first propagation wave reaches; `Reach` counts the transitive dependents a reachability cascade can visit. Their values on $I^*$ ($0.764$, $\rho_{>0} = 0.516$; $0.732$) therefore measure how much of the oracle a restatement of its rule recovers, not predictive skill. Exploratory raw-multigraph references are evaluated in the replication repository: counting published topics (Pubs-raw) reaches $0.731$, and transitive closure without Rule 5 (Reach-R1) $0.674$. Among the references, the derivation contributes in one measured place: without Rule 5, `Reach` falls by $0.058$ (9/12 folds, Holm $p = 0.0068$; derivation ablation). Untyped raw-graph metrics recover none of the reference: total degree reaches $0.199$ and reverse PageRank $0.089$, and PageRank is constant for every Application because Applications have no incoming raw edges.

**The first-order expansion of the oracle.** The strongest reference is a post hoc closed-form first-order expansion of $I^*$: $$\tag{6}
\hat{I}^*_1(v) = \sum_{t \in \text{pub}(v)} \frac{|\text{sub}(t)|}{|\text{pub}(t)|},$$ where $|\text{pub}(t)| \ge 1$ holds for all published topics $t \in \text{pub}(v)$ by construction ($v$ publishes to $t$), preventing any division by zero. It reaches mean $\rho = 0.808$ $[0.755, 0.853]$ ($\rho_{>0} = 0.631$): direct subscriber loss is most of what $I^*$ measures, and `InDeg` is a coarser version of the same quantity. As a reference it bounds the mean rather than every fold: on four folds (AV, Financial Trading, Healthcare, Industrial SCADA) `InDeg` slightly exceeds it (by $0.005$–$0.038$).

**Learners on the dependency graph approach the reference.** The same graph neural networks, trained on the dependency graph instead of the raw multigraph, reach $\rho = 0.748$ (`GAT-P-QoS`), the best GNN in Table 5, and $0.016$ below the direct-dependent count on the per-seed statistic; its five-seed ensemble is $0.007$ above it ($0.772$, Table 6). Neither difference is significant, and neither is equivalence: the $90\%$ interval of the per-fold difference reaches $\pm 0.076$, so a two one-sided test at the registered margin of $\pm 0.05$ fails ($p = 0.17$ per seed, $0.13$ for the ensemble; equivalence arm F3). The learner and the count are therefore indistinguishable at this sample size, not ranked. The approach to the reference rests on the degree features every learner receives (§3.5): removing `in_degree` and $w_{\text{in}}$ lowers `GAT-P-QoS` to $0.612$ (§6.2). A variant that uses `InDeg` as its prior is archived in the replication repository, because its prior is a reference. `HGT-P-QoS` reaches only $0.514$ and is highly seed-dependent (mean within-fold seed SD $0.254$; §7.3).

**Hybrids outperform the registered comparator, and little else.** Hybrid-HGT reaches $\rho = 0.657$ ($+0.103$, Holm $p = 0.0068$) and Hybrid-GAT $0.683$ ($+0.130$, Holm $p = 0.0029$) against `Topo-QoS`, each on 11 of 12 folds; both stay significant under the omnibus Holm correction ($p_{\text{omni}} = 0.041$ and $0.019$), under the Nadeau–Bengio corrected $t$ ($p = 0.019$ and $0.014$) and under $|V_{\text{app}}|$-weighted averaging (exact sign-flip $p = 0.038$ and $0.005$). They are the study’s only significant registered improvements, and they are weaker than they look, because a hybrid receives $p(v)$ as an input and adds a learned correction to its logit, so it nests its comparator. Against their own base learners, which were registered alongside, neither is significant (Hybrid-HGT vs. `HGT-QoS` $+0.035$, $p = 0.73$; Hybrid-GAT vs. `GAT-QoS` $+0.048$, $p = 0.30$). The prior also costs transfer: zero-shot, both hybrids fall below their base learners (§6.3). On their own, the raw-multigraph learned engines do not differ significantly from `Topo-QoS` (`HGT-QoS` $+0.069$, $p = 0.266$, the null primary contrast; `GAT-QoS` $+0.082$, $p = 0.233$).

**Beyond the reachability oracle.** Table 6 scores the rankers against the two other oracles. On full-population $I_{\text{dyn}}$, which agrees with $I^*$ at $\rho = 0.711$ $[0.625, 0.794]$, the references stay ahead of every GNN (first-order expansion $0.706$, `InDeg` $0.664$, against $0.496$–$0.615$), and `Topo-QoS` reaches $0.471$. Because $I_{\text{dyn}}$ shares the topology and the first-order effect with $I^*$, the partial correlation shows how much of each ranking survives outside the oracle it was fitted to or restates. After the rank of $I^*$ is removed, `InDeg` keeps $0.272$ $[0.188, 0.366]$, `Reach` $0.117$, `Topo-QoS` $0.134$ and the GNNs $0.107$–$0.173$, every interval excluding zero. After the first-order expansion is removed instead, the order reverses: `InDeg` keeps $0.079$, and the raw-multigraph GNNs keep most ($0.212$–$0.217$), so the learners carry queue-flow signal beyond the direct count that the count does not. Learning from the right labels matters more than the graph model. A gradient-boosted model over the counts, the first-order expansion and QoS features, trained on $I_{\text{dyn}}$ labels of the training scenarios, ranks held-out architectures at $0.799$, above the first-order expansion ($+0.094$, Holm $p = 0.019$) and `InDeg` ($+0.135$, Holm $p = 0.019$; Family B); its QoS features carry that gain ($+0.095$ over the same model without them, Holm $p = 0.014$), whereas on $I^*$ they add nothing ($-0.003$, $p = 0.62$; Family C). The GNN given the same labels does not: `GAT-P-QoS`$\to$dyn reaches $0.598$, below the first-order expansion ($-0.108$, Holm $p = 0.014$) and the gradient-boosted surrogate ($-0.201$, Holm $p = 0.0015$), and no better than the same GNN trained on $I^*$ ($-0.008$; control F5). Importantly, these two surrogates differ in their input representations as well as their model classes: the tree model was supplied with the analytical first-order expansion, declared publication rates, and message payload sizes alongside topological counts, whereas the GAT surrogate operated solely over topological graph features. While this confirms that gradient-boosted trees over domain-informed features distill queue-flow mechanics effectively, isolating the role of architectural inductive bias versus input feature disparity remains an open question requiring future feature-matched evaluations (§7.2). These surrogates are trained on the oracle they are scored on; their value is cost, since labeling $I_{\text{dyn}}$ took $12.7$ CPU-hours for the corpus (§6.4). Trained on $I^*$ instead, the same gradient-boosted model is less robust across the three oracles than the first-order expansion alone (worst-case $\rho$ $-0.121$, Holm $p = 0.001$; Family A).

**The multi-criteria oracle favors different rankers.** On $I_{\text{comp}}$, which adds fragmentation, throughput and flow disruption (§4.3), learned engines without a prior collapse ($0.144$–$0.334$; the sum-aggregation GNNs of §6.2 reach $0.41$–$0.44$), while the two hybrids, which carry `Topo-QoS` as a prior, keep $0.58$. Training-free scores proximate to that oracle’s terms rank higher: `Topo-QoS` reaches $0.702$ $[0.649, 0.753]$ (above the direct-dependent reference $0.650$; reference ahead on only 3 of 12 folds, $p = 0.110$), while raw total degree reaches $0.719$. `Reach` falls to $0.302$. Their lead is read as the same kind of overlap with $I_{\text{comp}}$’s fragmentation and flow terms as the references’ lead on $I^*$ (§4.4), not as predictive skill. No ranker is best on all three oracles.

**Identifying the critical set.** Rank correlation over all Applications is not the operational question in a CI gate, which is whether the true top of the ranking gets flagged. Figure 5 gives, for each ranker, the share of the true top-$20\%$ set on $I^*$ that falls within its top $k\%$, with ties resolved in expectation. At $k = 20\%$, `GAT-P-QoS` recovers $0.48$ of the critical set and `Topo-QoS` $0.38$, consistent with their Overlap@$K$ ($0.454$ and $0.388$): about half of what a top-$20\%$ gate flags is not in the true top $20\%$, and about half of the true top $20\%$ is missed. Recovering $80\%$ of the critical set requires flagging the top $40\%$ by `GAT-P-QoS` ($0.81$), and `Topo-QoS` does not reach $80\%$ within $50\%$. The references do little better: at $k = 20\%$, `InDeg` recovers $0.49$ (tie-breaking bounds $0.48$–$0.50$), and $80\%$ needs the top $45\%$ by `InDeg` ($0.83$) or by the first-order expansion ($0.89$); only the first-order expansion reaches $90\%$ within the top $50\%$, and `Reach` does not reach $80\%$ within $50\%$. On the earlier $n = 30$ $I_{\text{dyn}}$ developmental sample (the right panel of Figure 5; preserved for tie-breaking recall inspection; Table 6 reports the exhaustive $N = 1{,}321$ population), `GAT-P-QoS` reaches $0.65$ at $40\%$ and $0.80$ at $50\%$, against $0.83$ at $40\%$ for the `InDeg` reference. In an industrial deployment, requiring a gate to flag $40\%$ to $50\%$ of components to catch $80\%$ of critical points introduces substantial developer alert fatigue, indicating that SaG is most effective as a prioritized triage list for architectural inspection rather than an automated blocking gate.

## 6.2 RQ2: What Learned Engines Need

**Summary.** *Learned engines gain $+0.08$ to $+0.11$ from reading the dependency graph, where messages reach the scored Applications from their dependents (11–12 of 12 folds). What brings the dependency-graph GAT close to the reference is its degree features: without them it loses $0.14$ (Holm $p = 0.005$). A sum-aggregation GNN, which can count dependents, loses only $0.01$ without them, although the aggregator contrast itself is not significant after correction. With capacity matched, relation typing adds nothing; with the weighted in-degree held in both arms, the “QoS” inputs add nothing measurable either ($+0.030$, Holm $p = 0.33$).*

**What the dependency-graph learner needs.** Every learner receives in-degree and $w_{\text{in}}$ as node features, and on the dependency graph it also aggregates exactly the dependent neighborhood, so its closeness to `InDeg` could come from either. Ablation experiments separate the two (Table 8). Removing the two degree columns costs `GAT-P-QoS` $0.136$, and removing the three centralities most correlated with in-degree as well costs $0.161$ (F1, rule F1a): the approach to the reference depends on the degree features. That is what message-passing theory predicts for this architecture: softmax attention normalizes over neighbors and computes a weighted mean, which cannot count them, whereas a sum aggregator can [59, 60]. A GINE network of the same parameter budget bears this out descriptively, keeping $0.721$ and $0.711$ without the same columns, $0.01$–$0.02$ below its own full-feature score, but the aggregator contrasts miss significance after Holm correction ($+0.108$ and $+0.124$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.157$; rule F2b), so the study does not claim an aggregator effect. No dependency-graph learner, with or without degree features, is equivalent to `InDeg` within $\pm 0.05$ (equivalence bounds $0.063$–$0.276$; F3). Zero-shot, the degree columns matter little ($0.810$–$0.821$ without them), because on the system models most of the ranking is separating inert from active components (§6.3).

**Relation typing under matched capacity.** `HGT-QoS` remains within $0.013$ of `GAT-QoS` (Table 7). On the raw multigraph this says little about typing, because no message reaches an Application there: a gradient-boosted model over the same per-node features without any graph (`GBM-Feat`) reaches $0.642$, level with `GAT-QoS`, and removing HGT’s reverse pass, the only route by which messages reach Applications, changes nothing (`HGT-QoS-U`, $-0.010$, $p = 0.91$). On the dependency graph, where messages do arrive, `HGT-P-QoS` reaches $0.514$ with a mean within-fold seed SD of $0.254$, against $0.748$ and $0.030$ for `GAT-P-QoS`. This is one fixed configuration (width 100), and averaging its five seeds’ predictions raises it to $0.618$; we therefore read it as seed instability of that configuration, not as evidence against relational typing in general. **The registered selection rule.** The plan chose every hyperparameter by nested selection, which the published results did not apply (§5.3). Run afterwards on the stage-1 grid (arm N), it moves `HGT-QoS` from $0.622$ to $0.677$ ($+0.055$) and `GAT-P-QoS` from $0.748$ to $0.693$ ($-0.054$), neither significant (Holm $p = 0.259$); nested `HGT-QoS` against `Topo-QoS` is $+0.123$ on 9 of 12 folds, still not significant (Holm $p = 0.192$; rule F6b). Applying the registered rule therefore does not change the primary result. Table 5 reports the fixed, published configuration; Arm N is a registered secondary sensitivity analysis of it. The search harness also early-stops on a held-out training scenario rather than on the published node-level split, and on the three folds where it chose the published hyperparameters it does not reproduce the published scores (up to $0.10$ apart), so these differences cannot be attributed to hyperparameter selection alone.

## 6.3 RQ3: Zero-Shot Transfer to Models of Open-Source Systems

**Summary.** *On five hand-authored models of open-source systems, the best learned engines rank $I^*$ at about $0.81$ zero-shot (`GAT-P-QoS` $0.806$, `GAT-QoS` $0.805$), far above the training-free baseline ($0.526$), but their active-stratum agreement is weak ($\rho_{>0} \le 0.342$). The reference rankings reach higher (transitive dependents $0.938$, $\rho_{>0} = 0.871$; direct dependents $0.863$), because on these models most of $I^*$ is reachability itself. The models differ structurally from the synthetic corpus, and all five were written by one author, so these numbers describe transfer to those models, not to deployed systems. The baseline prior costs transfer: both hybrids fall below their base learners ($0.695$ and $0.662$ against $0.760$ and $0.805$).*

The `Reach` reference reaches $\rho = 0.938$ and $\rho_{>0} = 0.871$ on the five models: $0.836$–$0.997$ on the three originally publish–subscribe systems and $0.966$–$0.998$ on the two RPC systems re-expressed as pub-sub meshes. These values exceed anything `Reach` reaches on the synthetic folds ($0.732$, $\rho_{>0} = 0.286$), and the difference is structural. Half of the system models’ Applications have zero simulated impact ($0.51$ on average, against $0.31$ on the folds), and their fan-in is more concentrated (Gini $0.65$ against $0.50$). On such graphs, separating the inert half from the rest already produces a high full-population $\rho$, and `Reach`, which is zero exactly for components with no dependents, does that perfectly. The active-stratum $\rho_{>0}$ is the more informative column here. On it every predictor is weak (learned engines $0.185$–$0.342$, training-free baseline negative), as is the direct-dependent reference ($0.321$ $[-0.044, 0.687]$), while `Reach` ($0.871$) is far ahead: on these models, ordering active components by transitive reach is close to what $I^*$ computes, which is the circularity at its strongest, not transferable skill. Because all five models were hand-authored by a single modeler without independent cross-validation, and because two of them represent stylized pub-sub conversions of originally synchronous RPC systems (§5.1), these results confirm that learned rankers transfer coarse topological partitioning across handwritten model styles, but fail to deliver reliable within-cascade discrimination on unseen architectures.

## 6.4 RQ4: Analysis Cost and Sustainability

**Summary.** *Measured like for like, on the same graphs in one session (Table 10), one labeling pass of the reachability oracle over the Applications takes $0.01$–$0.72$ s per corpus architecture, the counting path that restates its first wave $17$–$176\times$ less (median $45\times$), and the feature extraction every learned engine needs $4.5$–$72.5\times$ more (median $16.9\times$). On generated graphs of up to $5{,}000$ components the ordering holds, with features at $5.7$–$18.7\times$ one pass. The queue-flow oracle is the expensive one: $12.7$ CPU-hours to label the corpus. Bounding energy consumption via CPU package power ($28$ W TDP) shows that lightweight surrogates save up to $\sim 355$ Wh compared to dynamic simulation, whereas for reachability, static counting remains the greenest choice.*

Table 10 replaces two comparisons earlier revisions got wrong. They set the full detection gate (system-layer analysis plus 18 anti-pattern detectors) against the five-seed labeling sweep over three node types and called that sweep “one run” of $I^*$ (median $5.6\times$), and they read earlier profiling sessions against different graphs. Timed together, every stage keeps its place in the ordering. The counting path is the cheapest, one $I^*$ pass over the scored population is next, and the app-layer feature extraction that the learned engines read is the most expensive, at $4.5$–$72.5\times$ one pass on the corpus. The articulation and Connectivity Degradation Index phase accounts for $88$–$91\%$ of it (Enterprise, and the $2{,}000$-component graph), and it is largest on Enterprise, whose Rule 1 projection is nearly complete. Where the $I^*$ ranking itself is wanted, running the oracle is therefore cheaper than approximating it with a learned engine at every size measured, and the count is cheaper still, by one to two orders of magnitude, because it restates the oracle’s first wave; its practical advantage is cost, not accuracy over the oracle it approximates. In the replication repository, timing profiles show that inference itself is negligible (tens of milliseconds at $2{,}000$ components), and that the count scales to $10{,}000$ components ($1.1$ s) and `Reach` less well ($23$ s), because it computes a transitive closure; complete timing artifacts and execution scripts are in `docs/research/jss/experiments/rq4-cost.md`. The expensive oracle is $I_{\text{dyn}}$: labeling the corpus took $12.7$ CPU-hours (about $7$ s per fault run and seed), which is the case for the learned surrogate of §6.1, whose inference runs in seconds. While direct hardware power counters (e.g., RAPL or physical wall meters) were not instrumented, we can bound the computational energy expenditure using the host CPU’s base package power ($28.0$ W TDP for the 13th Gen Intel Core i7-1370P; `reproduce/energy_estimate.py`). Under this upper-bound model, generating labels for the $12.7$ CPU-hour $I_{\text{dyn}}$ simulation expended up to $\sim 355.6$ Wh ($\approx 1.28$ MJ). In contrast, executing static feature extraction across the entire corpus required only $106.9$ s (an upper bound of $0.83$ Wh / $2.99$ kJ), and computing the training-free counting baseline took just $11.1$ s total ($0.086$ Wh / $310$ J). From a Green Software Engineering and Green CI/CD perspective [21, 22, 23], deploying heavy neural architectures or full dynamic simulations in continuous integration represents a high-overhead, energy-intensive path. Learning pays its computational and carbon footprint only where it displaces repeated executions of expensive simulation oracles like $I_{\text{dyn}}$, providing a $19\times$ speedup and massive energy savings, whereas for cheap reachability analysis, simple static graph queries remain the strictly greener choice.
