# 9. Conclusion

Deriving explicit dependencies from architecture models is what makes cascading-failure risk in publish–subscribe systems rankable before deployment. SaG turns an architecture into a typed multigraph and, through six publish–subscribe rules, into a graph of logical dependencies that exposes the paths along which failures travel. On twelve held-out synthetic architectures, counting a component’s dependents on that graph ranks its simulated cascade impact at $\rho = 0.764$, above closed-form centrality on every fold, while counting its raw connections reaches only $0.199$. On independently authored models of five open-source systems, counting transitive dependents reaches $\rho = 0.938$ with no training at all. The same graph lifts every other analyzer: centrality on the Application–Library dependency graph gains $+0.204$ on every fold, and graph neural networks gain $+0.075$ to $+0.113$ when they read it instead of the raw multigraph, reaching $\rho = 0.748$, level with the dependency counts. Hybrid engines, in which a learned model corrects closed-form centrality, beat it on 11 of 12 folds under decision rules registered before their runs, and remain significant under a Holm correction over all thirteen confirmatory contrasts. Learned engines need messages from a component’s dependents and declared coupling inputs, not relation-specific parameters, so a simple untyped attention network suffices, and trained only on synthetic data they transfer far above centrality ($0.760$–$0.831$ against $0.511$–$0.526$).

For practice, the most valuable step is to model the architecture with typed entities and derive its dependencies. A CI gate can then count dependents in milliseconds, `InDeg` for familiar architectures and `Reach` for unfamiliar ones, and a learned engine that reads the dependency graph can be added where richer inputs are available. SaG’s ISO/IEC 25010 layer names the remediation each flagged component calls for. Because the synthetic corpus regenerates byte-identically, every registered decision rule is applied as written, and every reported value is reconciled against released artifacts, others can extend or challenge these results directly.

Three steps would widen their reach: retargeting the counts and the engines on the behavioral queue-flow oracle, and on oracles that express QoS semantics such as deadline misses, where learned corrections could exceed the counts; independent re-models of the five systems; and extracting models from deployment manifests, so that rankings can be validated against production incidents.
