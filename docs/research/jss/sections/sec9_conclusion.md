# 8. Conclusion

Software-as-a-Graph (SaG) derives explicit dependency graphs from publish–subscribe deployment manifests and ranks components by simulated cascade impact before deployment. We used it to benchmark closed-form centrality, graph neural networks, hybrid engines and learned surrogates on twelve synthetic architectures and five hand-authored models of open-source systems, against three simulators.

The registered primary contrast, a heterogeneous graph transformer against weighted centrality, was null, and it stays null under the plan’s own selection rule. Two hybrids beat centrality on 11 of 12 synthetic architectures but not their own base learners. The reachability simulator’s first propagation wave is exactly a component’s number of direct dependents, so dependency counts were reported as references that restate the oracle, not as predictors: its first-order expansion reached $\rho = 0.808$ and the direct-dependent count $0.764$. Graph attention networks reading the dependency graph reached $0.748$, statistically indistinguishable from that count but not equivalent to it, and they get there through the degree features they are given. On a multi-criteria simulator, centrality and raw degree, both proximate to its terms, ranked above every learned engine. No predictor identifies the critical set sharply: catching $80\%$ of the true top fifth requires flagging the top $40\%$ even for the best learned engine.

For practice, the evidence supports a two-part recommendation. Where the oracle is cheap, as reachability is, run it; publish–subscribe afferent coupling is a cheaper stand-in for it, and learned engines add nothing measurable over either. Where the oracle is expensive, as the queue-flow simulator is, a learned surrogate trained on its labels is worth having: it ranked held-out architectures above every closed-form approximation, and there, unlike on reachability, declared QoS contracts carried signal. Whether any of these rankings, or the simulators themselves, predict real outages remains the open question, and benchmark deployments with catalogued faults are where it can be answered.
