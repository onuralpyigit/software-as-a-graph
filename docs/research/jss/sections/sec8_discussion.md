# 7. Discussion, Threats to Validity, and Limitations

## 7.1 Dependency Representation versus Model Complexity

The central finding of this study is that, for the reachability and queue-flow simulators, explicit dependency representations contributed more to ranking performance than model complexity. Deriving a dependency graph from publish–subscribe deployment manifests enabled both analytical and learned approaches: learners gained $+0.08$ to $+0.11$ on it compared with the raw multigraph, and rankings computed directly from it, such as the direct-dependent count ($0.764$) and the rate-weighted first-order approximation ($0.830$), matched or exceeded the best learned models ($0.748$ and $0.799$). Pure learners on the raw multigraph did not outperform the training-free baseline, because messages never reached the scored components. Explicit dependency derivation is therefore not merely a preprocessing step for graph learning; in the evaluated corpus, it is the principal source of predictive signal. The multi-criteria simulator is the exception: there, raw degree and betweenness, whose terms align with fragmentation, ranked highest.

## 7.2 When Learning Adds Value, and Relative to What

The study does not show that learning is never useful; it shows where learning adds value and against which comparator. Relative to the training-free baseline, learning helped in two ways. First, hybrids that correct a closed-form prior significantly outperformed that baseline ($+0.103$ and $+0.130$, 11 of 12 folds), and in an exploratory comparison they were the most consistent learned models across the three simulators. Second, learned models transferred zero-shot to unfamiliar architectures better than the baseline ($\approx 0.81$ vs. $0.53$). Relative to analytical references built on the same dependencies, however, no learned model showed additional skill. The references matched or exceeded the learned models in-distribution and ranked higher in transfer ($0.863$–$0.938$), and ordering among components that actually propagate failures remained weak for every learned model ($\rho_{>0} \le 0.342$). Learning is therefore likely to contribute most where impact depends on mechanisms that no available analytical approximation captures, a condition this corpus did not exercise. A per-regime breakdown of these results by architecture type is archived in the replication repository (`results/engine_regimes.json`; Supplementary §S34).

**How message passing contributes.** On the raw multigraph, learning mainly used node properties. Three checks explain how these engines worked. First, gradient-boosted decision trees using only per-node features (no graph message passing, `GBM-Feat`) reach $\rho = 0.642$ under LOSO, similar to `GAT-QoS` ($0.635$). Second, in the raw multigraph, all structural links point away from Applications, so standard forward GAT convolutions do not send messages to the scored nodes—removing all edges does not change their outputs. Third, HGT reaches Applications only through reverse edge passes, and removing them has no effect (`HGT-QoS-U`, $-0.010$, $p = 0.91$). So, on the raw multigraph, neural models acted as non-linear combiners of precomputed centralities and degree metrics, not as relational message-passing engines. On the derived dependency graph, directed edges go from dependents to dependencies: three layers of message passing gather information from the true dependent neighborhood, letting `GAT-P-QoS` outperform `GBM-Feat` by $+0.106$. Since every learner also gets an in-degree feature (§3.5), this margin does not mean attention finds the dependent count on its own, and ablation tests show it does not: without the degree columns, `GAT-P-QoS` loses $0.136$, while a sum-aggregation network of the same size loses $0.011$ (§6.2).

**Homogeneous versus heterogeneous architectures.** On the raw multigraph, where message passing into Applications was inactive, adding typing made no measurable difference (main effect $-0.014$, interaction $+0.001$). On the dependency graph, where message passing is active, the heterogeneous transformer (`HGT-P-QoS`) was unstable across seeds in the tested configuration (width 100, fixed setup; mean within-fold seed standard deviation $0.254$, mean $\rho = 0.514$), while the homogeneous `GAT-P-QoS` was stable ($0.030$, $\rho = 0.748$). This means that, in this setup, relation-specific parameters did not improve accuracy, though they reduced stability. This does not mean typing is always harmful. Only `HGT-QoS` was run under the registered selection rule (§6.2); averaging five seeds’ predictions raises `HGT-P-QoS` to $0.618$ (still below the references), and denser topologies or architectures with messages reaching scored nodes may behave differently.

**Why the learned queue-flow approximation does not exceed the formula.** The gradient-boosted approximation (`GBM-P-QoS→dyn`, $0.799$) outperforms the unweighted first-order expansion ($0.706$) because it reads declared publication rates and payload sizes, which $I_{\text{dyn}}$ uses and the unweighted expansion ignores. Once rates enter the analytical expansion (Eq. 7), it reaches $0.830$ and matches or exceeds the learned approximation. The attention-based approximation (`GAT-P-QoS→dyn`, $0.598$) received neither rates nor payloads, and it scores within $0.008$ of the same model trained on $I^*$ (control F5). Whether a learned model given the same inputs as the formula, or trained to correct it, can exceed the formula is left to future work (§7.6).

## 7.3 Restatement versus Prediction

Several of the highest-scoring rankings largely restate a simulator’s propagation rule: the direct-dependent count and the first-order expansion for the reachability simulator, and the rate-weighted first-order approximation for the queue-flow simulator. The reference criterion (§4.4) distinguishes recovering a simulator’s definition from predicting its outcomes, and it changes how both analytical and learned results should be read. A learned model that approaches a reference has learned the simulator’s first-order rule, not necessarily anything beyond it. Because simulation-labelled benchmarks are common in architecture analysis, the criterion offers a general check for evaluations of this kind: before crediting a learned model with predictive skill, compare it against the simplest ranking that restates the simulator’s own computation.

## 7.4 Practical Consequences

**Choosing a ranker by simulator cost.** The usefulness of a ranker depends on how costly the simulator it approximates is. If the simulator is inexpensive, like the reachability simulator $I^*$ ($0.01$–$0.72$ s per pass over a corpus architecture’s Applications; §6.4), practitioners can compute its ranking directly from the manifest. Publish–subscribe afferent coupling, a direct-dependent count used in architecture research for two decades [47, 48], matches any graph neural network tested at a fraction of the cost; because it restates the simulator’s first wave (§7.3), its value is cost, not predictive skill. If the simulator is expensive, like the queue-flow simulator $I_{\text{dyn}}$ ($12.7$ CPU-hours to label the corpus), a rate-weighted first-order expansion computed from declared publication rates approximates it at $\rho = 0.830$ in milliseconds, matching or exceeding a gradient-boosted approximation trained on its labels ($0.799$). This study does not show that either simulator, or any of its approximations, predicts real outages.

**Choosing a ranker by failure notion.** Table 11 summarizes the evidence by oracle. Practitioners must interpret these recommendations as conditional on simulation fidelity. If the discrete-event or cascade simulator does not capture specific physical failure modes (e.g., partial network partitions or disk thrashing), ranking efficacy on physical clusters could diverge. On the reachability oracle $I^*$, the best learned engine is the dependency-graph GAT (`GAT-P-QoS`, $0.748$), followed by the hybrids ($0.657$–$0.683$); all require training, and none is statistically distinguishable from the direct-dependent reference ($0.764$). Where the reachability ranking itself is wanted, running $I^*$ directly is the simplest and cheapest option at corpus size. Where the queue-flow notion of impact matters ($I_{\text{dyn}}$), the rate-weighted first-order expansion ($0.830$) is the recommended ranker; it matches or exceeds the learned approximation trained on queue-flow labels ($0.799$), and this is the only simulator on which declared information carries measurable signal beyond the dependency structure, from publication rates and payload sizes rather than from QoS policies or QoS-weighted scores. Where failure involves fragmentation and throughput loss ($I_{\text{comp}}$), training-free topological scores (`Topo-QoS`, $\rho = 0.702$) rank highest because their betweenness and articulation terms directly align with fragmentation; among learned models, pure neural rankers collapse ($\le 0.334$), while hybrids retain robustness ($\rho = 0.585$). On unfamiliar topologies like the five system models, learned engines transfer zero-shot at about $0.81$ against $0.526$ for the training-free baseline. Still, active-stratum agreement is weak ($\rho_{>0} \le 0.342$), and the result rests on five models by one author.

**Mitigating Alert Fatigue with a Two-Tiered Triage Protocol.** Using a top-20% threshold on the direct-dependent count (`InDeg`) recovers about half of the true top-20% set on $I^*$ ($0.49$), while the rest of the flagged items are not in that set; the best learned ranker does no better (`GAT-P-QoS`, $0.48$). Figure 5 shows the trade-off: recovering 80% of the critical set takes the top 45% by `InDeg` ($0.83$) or the top 40% by `GAT-P-QoS` ($0.81$), `Topo-QoS` does not reach this level within the top 50%, and no predictor reaches 90% within the top 50%. To catch most critical components, a gate would need to flag nearly half the system, making these rankers unsuitable as automated pass/fail filters. In a system with over 100 services, flagging 40–50 would overwhelm developers with alerts.

To operationalize these rankings constructively, we recommend a *two-tiered triage protocol* rather than an automated blocking gate:

1.  **Tier 1 (Commit-stage triage):** When reviewing pull requests, compute the direct-dependent count (about 11 ms, an estimated 0.086 Wh) and the rate-weighted first-order expansion (Eq. 7; about a millisecond). Show the top 5 components by each as an informational note in the pull request review. This highlights potential cascade risks for peer review without blocking developers or causing alert fatigue.

2.  **Tier 2 (Staging and sprint verification):** During nightly builds or sprint architecture reviews, run the simulators on the Tier-1 shortlist within a fixed inspection budget, such as the ten highest-ranked services, and reserve redundant broker routing or chaos fault injection for the components the simulation confirms.

**Cost and Green CI/CD.** As shown in Table 10, the count is the cheapest ranker, followed by a single pass of $I^*$. Feature extraction for learned models is the most expensive, costing 4.5 to 72.5 times as much as a single pass on the corpus. Where the reachability ranking is wanted, running $I^*$ or counting dependents is simpler and cheaper than any learned approximation. The same holds for the queue-flow simulator, where the rate-weighted expansion replaces an estimated $355.6$ Wh ($12.7$ CPU-hours at $28$ W TDP) of simulation for the corpus with a computation of milliseconds. From a Green CI/CD perspective [23, 22, 21], training-free dependency analysis is therefore the lower-energy choice at both commit and staging time. The energy figures are estimates derived from processor TDP, not measurements (§7.5).

## 7.5 Threats to Validity

**Construct validity and oracle circularity.** All ground truth is simulated, and no ranker is validated against observed failures in physical deployments. We conducted no empirical fault injection (e.g., chaotic network partitions, process crashes, or disk exhaustion) on running distributed clusters. Because all rankers and references are evaluated against simulator-generated labels, the findings establish what can be predicted within the evaluated simulation framework, not what necessarily predicts real-world failures; external validation remains necessary. Discrete-event simulations omit complex real-world distributed runtime dynamics: kernel TCP buffer bloat, socket starvation, thread pool exhaustion, garbage collection freezes, asymmetric network partition drops, and cascading retry storms. The circularity between the primary oracle and the rankers is analyzed in §4.4: the dependency counts are references for $I^*$, the rate-weighted expansion is a reference for $I_{\text{dyn}}$, closed-form centrality is proximate to $I_{\text{comp}}$, and the learned engines inherit part of the overlap through their degree features, which ablation experiments show they depend on. Inert components are separated by construction, which is why $\rho_{>0}$ is reported beside $\rho$. We decided to reclassify the counts as references after all results were finalized, and this change did not affect any numbers. The two other oracles bound, but do not remove, the threat: $I_{\text{dyn}}$ shares the topology and the first-order effect, and $I_{\text{comp}}$’s weights encode a declared rather than an elicited priority vector. No learned ranker exceeded the analytical references on any simulator: the learned queue-flow approximation outperformed only the unweighted expansion, and the rate-weighted expansion, itself a reference for $I_{\text{dyn}}$, matched or exceeded it. The evaluation is confined to Applications, so dependency Rules 2–4 and 6, which govern brokers and hosts, are untested.

**Internal validity, reproducibility, and drift.** Predictors consume $G_{\text{analysis}}$ while simulation oracles execute on $G_{\text{structural}}$, verified by regression tests. We held model capacity, depth, and early stopping constant across comparison arms. The registered selection rule was not applied to the published learned results, a deviation disclosed in §5.3; a post hoc sensitivity check applies it to `HGT-QoS` and `GAT-P-QoS` (§6.2), and every other statement regarding a learned engine is conditional on one fixed configuration. The learned engines are also seed-sensitive. Across the five seeds of a fold, the mean within-fold standard deviation of $\rho$ is $0.099$ for `HGT-QoS`, $0.254$ for `HGT-P-QoS`, $0.024$ for `GAT-QoS` and $0.030$ for `GAT-P-QoS`, and single `HGT` seeds differ from others in the same fold by more than $1.0$. Averaging the five seeds’ *predictions* instead of their $\rho$ values raises every learned engine, most of all the unstable ones (`HGT-P-QoS` from $0.514$ to $0.618$); the tables report the registered statistic, the mean of per-seed $\rho$. Across revision cycles and compute devices, learned cells drifted by up to $0.172$ ($\pm 0.041$ on `HGT-QoS`), more than several effects this paper reports (the primary contrast, $+0.069$; the QoS main effect, $+0.073$). For that reason, every learned cell in Tables 5, 7 and 9 is reported from one named CPU sweep whose per-seed logs reproduce the published mean exactly, the sensitivity verification sweep re-ran every learned comparator to an exact match, and a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples seeds within folds widens the learned rows’ intervals only slightly. The Connectivity Degradation Index samples nodes with hash-dependent tie-breaking, so `PYTHONHASHSEED=0` is fixed. We reconcile all reported values mechanically against released artifacts.

**External validity and the single-modeller threat.** Synthetic topologies come from a single generator family, which may induce parametric regularities not representative of heterogeneous industrial architectures. The five system models are hand-authored approximations (22–41 Applications) of open-source projects, each written by the first author from public documentation. All five RQ3 results are exposed to this threat: no second modeler authored any model independently, and no inter-modeler reliability metric is reported. To establish a baseline for future multi-rater replication, we conducted an exploratory calibration spot-check on the two smallest systems (Home Assistant and EdgeX Foundry), evaluating node-level Jaccard overlap and edge concordance between the primary model and a structured re-extraction by the second author. While service entity identification showed high agreement ($J \ge 0.88$), edge multiplicity and topic topology diverged ($J \approx 0.65$–$0.72$), showing that broker channel synthesis from documentation requires non-trivial modeler interpretation. The system models also differ structurally from the synthetic folds: a larger share of their Applications has zero simulated impact, and their fan-in is more concentrated, favoring `Reach` and inflating full-population $\rho$. Furthermore, two of the five (Train-Ticket and Online Boutique) are synchronous RPC systems re-expressed as asynchronous publish–subscribe meshes. This translation maps request–reply call graphs into topic pub-sub topologies, which may introduce semantic distortions into dependency relations. We did not evaluate architectures with tens of thousands of components; we timed the counting path up to $10{,}000$ components (§6.4), and we did not evaluate the learned engines or the oracles. We did not reproduce published learned criticality models (FINDER, DrBC) because they do not support typed multigraphs or pub-sub semantics.

**Sustainability and energy estimation validity.** Energy metrics reported for continuous integration workflows (§6.4) are estimates derived from processor thermal design power (28.0W TDP) multiplied by single-threaded execution duration (`reproduce/energy_estimate.py`). They do not capture hardware-level power variations such as dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS), DRAM or disk energy, or multi-socket server idle power, and they were not verified with physical hardware meters (e.g., RAPL or external power analyzers). While sufficient for relative orders-of-magnitude engineering trade-offs, they should not be construed as physical ground-truth carbon emissions. Because the processor can exceed its TDP under turbo boost, these figures are not strict upper bounds.

**Conclusion validity.** The only confirmatory results are the plan’s two contrasts, the null primary and the null secondary (§5.3). Everything else, including the two hybrid contrasts, is registered secondary or exploratory and was designed after the primary null was known. LOSO folds share ten of eleven training scenarios, so the Wilcoxon $p$-values are nominal and anti-conservative; the Nadeau–Bengio corrected test, which accounts for the overlap, leaves the hybrid contrasts significant and the primary null. For training-free rankers, the folds are simply twelve scenarios from one generator, so the effective sample is small, and with twelve folds a bootstrap CI over folds is coarse; over five system models it is indicative only. Equivalence, which a claim that one ranker “matches” another requires, is not established for any pair at $\pm 0.05$.

## 7.6 Limitations and Future Work

Four directions follow from the study’s central question, in order of priority:

1.  **Validation against real failures:** controlled fault injection on ROS 2, Kafka and MQTT deployments (for example with Chaos Mesh or ChaosBlade), reference systems with known faults such as Train-Ticket [33], and incident data from industrial publish–subscribe systems.

2.  **Independent modelers:** replication of the five system models by additional modelers, with inter-modeler agreement reported, extending the spot-check in §7.5.

3.  **Alternative impact models:** testing whether the conclusions hold for simulators with different failure mechanisms, including synchronous call semantics, which the current dependency rules do not model.

4.  **When learning exceeds analytical methods:** identifying impact-generation mechanisms whose predictive signal is not captured by explicit dependency structure. This includes learned corrections on top of the rate-weighted approximation, and learned approximations that receive the same rate and payload information as the analytical formulas.

Further extensions are automatic extraction of architecture graphs from ROS 2 launch files, Kubernetes manifests and Helm charts; hardware-measured energy accounting (for example via RAPL); a full hyperparameter search for every learned model; and validation of the proposed explanation layer.
