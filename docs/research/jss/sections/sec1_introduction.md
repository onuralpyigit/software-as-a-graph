# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Motivation and Problem

Large distributed systems increasingly communicate through asynchronous publish–subscribe (pub-sub) middleware: ROS 2 in autonomous driving [1], Apache Kafka in enterprise event streams [2], DDS in cyber-physical systems [3], MQTT in IoT [4], and cloud-native microservices [5, 6]. Pub-sub decouples producers and consumers in space, time and synchronization [7]. Components interact through topics and brokers rather than direct references, and deployment-time Quality-of-Service (QoS) policies govern reliability, durability, priority and deadlines.

The same decoupling hides how failures spread. Publishers and subscribers share no direct link, so outages, head-of-line blocking and backpressure propagate along concealed paths through brokers, shared topics, colocated hosts and shared libraries [8, 9]. These failures take two forms. In *sequential cascades*, a slow subscriber fills a broker queue and gradually starves its publishers [10]. In *simultaneous blasts*, a shared-library crash or host outage takes down every colocated service at once. Neither architecture diagrams nor static call graphs show these mechanisms. The cheapest time to reduce the risk is before deployment, at design and continuous-integration time [11, 12], when no runtime telemetry exists. Architects therefore need to know, from configuration manifests alone, which components, topics and links are systemically critical and why.

Existing practice leaves this gap open, which we call the **Architecture–Code Gap**: a system can have bug-free code in every service and still be fragile through hidden single points of failure or mismatched QoS contracts [13, 14]. Architecture evaluations such as the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) rely on manual elicitation [15]. Static code analysis inspects services in isolation [16, 17]. Chaos engineering [18] needs a provisioned cluster, while pre-production alternatives like service-level fault injection testing [19] and microservice dependency tracing [20] require runnable execution environments. In continuous integration pipelines, heavy fault injection or cluster-based chaos experiments also incur significant computational and energy costs, making pre-deployment static analysis an attractive, green alternative if its accuracy is sufficient [21, 22, 23]. Homogeneous centrality flattens typed topologies into untyped graphs [24, 25]. Learned models, graph neural networks in particular, could combine these structural cues, but only if they read a graph on which failure paths are explicit. What is missing is such a representation for pub-sub systems, and evidence on what learning achieves on it: which learned rankers work, what they need, and whether they add anything over the coupling metrics that architecture research already has. Furthermore, in practice, architects require an actionable triage and review prioritization mechanism rather than an automated, brittle pass/fail gate that risks developer alert fatigue. This paper provides that evidence for one well-defined task, ranking components by simulated cascade impact before deployment.

## 1.2 The Software-as-a-Graph (SaG) Approach

**Software-as-a-Graph (SaG)** is a pre-deployment static analysis framework for event-driven architectures (Figure 1). It (1) models an architecture as a typed, directed multigraph over Applications, Brokers, Topics, Execution Hosts and Shared Libraries (§3.1); (2) derives from it, through publish–subscribe rules, an explicit `DEPENDS_ON` dependency graph (§3.3); and (3) evaluates learned cascade-impact rankers—graph neural networks on the raw multigraph and on the dependency graph, hybrids, and learned surrogates, against a training-free baseline (§§4 and 5.2). Predictors read only the analysis graph. Ground truth comes from three simulators that run on the raw structural topology (§4.3).

**What the ground truth is, and is not.** All ground truth in this study is simulated. The primary oracle $I^*$ is a reachability simulation that runs on the same manifest the predictors read, and its first propagation wave is exactly the number of a component’s direct dependents (§4.4). Dependency counts are therefore reported as references that restate the oracle, never as predictors. The study measures ranking agreement between learned rankers and algorithmic failure simulators, not the prediction of observed production outages. Because ranking against $I^*$ rewards restating its propagation rule, we bound the circularity with two further simulators and with partial correlations, and report the cost of running $I^*$ itself (§6.4); validation against observed failures on physical deployments remains an open challenge (§7.3). The practical conclusions and guidance derived here therefore hold conditionally under the assumption of simulation fidelity.

**Findings in brief.** Where graph learning operates on the derived dependency graph, it achieves strong cascade-impact prediction: graph attention networks gain $+0.08$ to $+0.11$ over the raw multigraph, reaching Spearman $\rho = 0.748$ against the reachability oracle $I^*$, and transfer zero-shot across five stylized models inspired by open-source systems at $\rho \approx 0.81$ ($\text{PR-AUC} = 0.838$), although within-cascade active-stratum discrimination collapses ($\rho_{>0} \le 0.342$). A critical discovery is that this high reachability performance aligns closely with the number of direct dependents ($0.764$, publish–subscribe afferent coupling [26, 27]), which restates the reachability oracle’s first propagation wave (first-order expansion $0.808$). The learner distills this fundamental topological signal through its degree features (losing $0.14$ without them), demonstrating that classical architectural coupling provides a near-instant, zero-carbon triage baseline. On high-fidelity dynamic queue-flow simulation ($I_{\text{dyn}}$) whose labels cost $12.7$ CPU-hours (an upper-bound theoretical proxy of $\sim 355.6$ Wh based on TDP), learned models deliver their strongest advantage: a gradient-boosted surrogate over dependency-graph features and declared rate/payload inputs ranks held-out architectures at $\rho = 0.799$, significantly outperforming every closed-form approximation ($0.706$, Holm $p = 0.019$), and declared QoS contracts carry measurable signal ($+0.095$). When failure involves multi-criteria structural fragmentation ($I_{\text{comp}}$), hybrid engines combining neural models with topological priors maintain robust stability ($\rho = 0.585$) where pure neural models collapse ($\le 0.334$). On the raw multigraph where forward messages do not reach Applications, the registered primary contrast of a heterogeneous graph transformer against the training-free baseline was null ($+0.069$, $p = 0.266$). Feature extraction for learned ranking costs $4.5$–$72.5\times$ one reachability simulation pass. Consequently, learning pays decisively where simulation is computationally expensive, establishing a sustainable two-tier deployment architecture.

## 1.3 Research Questions

-   **RQ1 (Ranking accuracy):** *How accurately do learned engines (graph neural networks on the raw multigraph and on the dependency graph, hybrids, and learned surrogates) rank components by cascading-failure impact on unseen architectures, against a training-free baseline, and how close do they come to reference rankings that restate the oracle’s propagation rule?*

-   **RQ2 (What learning needs):** *What do learned engines need: which graph they read, their degree features, relation-specific (typed) parameters, or QoS inputs, once model capacity and edge-channel width are matched?*

-   **RQ3 (Transfer):** *How well do learned engines trained on synthetic architectures transfer zero-shot to stylized architectural models hand-authored by a single modeler based on five open-source systems, and does within-cascade discrimination hold on the active stratum?*

-   **RQ4 (Cost and Sustainability):** *What does learned ranking cost at CI/CD time in latency and computational energy, which stage dominates, and how does it compare with running the simulation directly?*

The evaluation follows a version-controlled analysis plan. Only the plan’s two contrasts are confirmatory; the matched $2\times2$, the hybrids, the reference counts, the dependency-graph learners, the full-population queue-flow labels and the sensitivity arms were registered as planned extensions, each before its own arms ran but after the primary result was known, and are reported as registered secondary results; the rest is exploratory (§5.3). One deviation from the plan, its unapplied selection rule, is disclosed and tested. The replication repository documents the revision history and maps these four research questions onto the plan.

## 1.4 Contributions

Evaluated under leave-one-scenario-out (LOSO) cross-validation over twelve synthetic architectures and on five hand-authored stylized models of open-source systems, this paper contributes:

1.  **The Software-as-a-Graph (SaG) framework and multi-oracle evaluation suite** (§§5–6): seventeen architectures (2,812 components across twelve synthetic scenarios and five stylized system models), three distinct simulation oracles labelled for every Application, graph neural networks on the raw multigraph and on the dependency graph, hybrid engines and learned surrogates, supported by a version-controlled analysis plan, committed data manifests, and 1,562 byte-identically verified figures.

2.  **High-accuracy cascade prediction and causal demystification of graph learning mechanics** (§§6.1–6.2): Graph attention networks on the derived dependency graph achieve $\rho = 0.748$, gaining $+0.08$ to $+0.11$ over raw multigraphs; causal ablations show that their performance rests on distilling degree features, while sum aggregation naturally counts neighbors, explaining why classical afferent coupling provides a zero-cost triage baseline for reachability.

3.  **A high-performance dynamic surrogate for Green CI/CD** (§6.1): A gradient-boosted surrogate trained on dynamic queue-flow labels using analytical first-order expansion, rate, and payload features alongside topological counts reaches $\rho = 0.799$ on held-out architectures, significantly outperforming closed-form approximations ($+0.094$, $p = 0.019$) and amortizing $12.7$ CPU-hours ($\sim 355.6$ Wh theoretical TDP proxy) of discrete-event simulation into sub-second inference.

4.  **Resilient hybrid architectures under multi-criteria fragmentation** (§6.1): Demonstrating that combining neural models with domain priors prevents catastrophic ranking collapse under structural fragmentation and flow disruption ($\rho = 0.585$ vs. $\le 0.334$).

5.  **Explicit graph derivation rules for publish–subscribe architectures** (§3): Extending the application-level rule of [28] with library-mediated blast radii, host colocation, and middleware QoS edge encodings.

6.  **A like-for-like computational cost and sustainability analysis** (§6.4): Measuring exact latencies and energy footprints across counting, simulation, and learned inference, establishing an actionable two-tier triage pipeline for continuous integration to mitigate developer alert fatigue.

A previous conference paper [28] introduced the publish–subscribe multigraph, the Application-level subscriber$\to$publisher dependency (Rule 1 of Table 2) and a closed-form betweenness–articulation score of the same form as the training-free baseline (Eq. 5, unweighted and with weights $0.7$/$0.3$), and validated that score against a reachability-loss simulation on synthetic topologies and two ROS 2 benchmarks. Everything else is new in this paper: the Library and infrastructure rules (Rules 2–6, including library-mediated blasts), QoS weighting, the reference counts, the learned and hybrid engines, the QoS edge encoding, LOSO and zero-shot evaluation, the second and third oracles, the matched control and the cost profile.
