Simulate Walkthrough

Simulation fails components on the declared architecture and measures the damage. It does two separate jobs: it writes the labels a predictor is trained on, and it acts as the referee a prediction is validated against. Different engines do each job, and they are never swapped. This page follows both jobs on a 15-component air-traffic system.

Job 1 · labels · 1 · the artifact

The fault injector fails each labelled component in turn, over five seeds, and records how much of their input the subscribers lose. What it writes is not just a score per component: the file carries enough provenance to be checked before anyone trains on it.

Job 1 · labels · 2 · the options

Three settings change what a label means

The published labels use one fixed configuration. The other settings exist for sensitivity studies and ablations, and a label made with them is a different quantity. Pick a setting to see how the labels move.

Job 1 · labels · 3 · before training

A label file that looks fine can still be wrong for the job: measured on another topology, written by the other engine, missing the label asked for, or too noisy to reproduce. The prediction step runs these checks and refuses to train rather than warn. Here the same checks are offered one good artifact and four bad ones.

Job 2 · referee · 4 · one at a time

The validation oracle fails every application, broker and host

The failure simulator scores each failure as system-wide loss: severed paths, fragmentation, lost throughput and broken flows. This is the number Q(v) is validated against.

Job 2 · referee · 5 · together

Failures that happen together

Real outages are rarely one component. The oracle can fail every pair, or named groups that share a cause.

Job 2 · referee · 6 · uncertainty and links

Repeated trials, and the cost of losing a single link

When cascades are probabilistic, repeated trials give a distribution instead of a number. Removing one relationship while both ends stay up measures what that link carries.
